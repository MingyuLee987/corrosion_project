Abstract

In this study, Q370qENH high-strength weathering bridge steel was used as the base material. The corrosion experiment in a marine atmosphere was simulated by the salt spray test, and the outdoor atmospheric exposure corrosion experiment and electrochemical method test were carried out. The corrosion behavior of Q370qENH high-strength weathering bridge steel in a marine atmosphere was studied using electron probe microanalysis (EPMA), field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and other surface testing techniques. The results show that the corrosion rate of the steel gradually decreases with the increase in the content of trace rare earth elements. Ce played a role in the modification of inclusions so that MnS was modified into rare earth composite inclusions, which slowed down the occurrence of corrosion. The enrichment of Cu alloy elements in the inner rust layer of the rare earth experimental steel improves the compactness of the rust layer, and the thickness of the inner rust layer is increased by 42%, which enhances the stability of the rust layer. With the increase in cerium, the protection coefficient α/γ* of the rust layer of experimental steel increases, indicating that the corrosion resistance of the material is improved. In addition, the electrochemical results show that the addition of rare earth elements in Q370qENH steel will lead to a positive shift in the electrochemical self-corrosion potential, a larger impedance radius of the steel rust layer, and a stronger protective effect. Due to the addition of trace cerium, the seawater corrosion resistance of the test steel is improved.
Keywords:
rare earth elements; atmospheric exposure; corrosion weight loss; corrosion products; galvanic corrosion

1. Introduction

Weather-resistant bridge steel, as an important infrastructure material, is often used in some coastal areas, such as cross-sea bridges and dual-purpose steel bridges for highways and railroads across the gulf, which are exposed to the marine atmosphere. The air contains a high concentration of chloride ions, and steel is susceptible to corrosion, which can lead to safety problems and economic losses. At present, many methods have been studied, including coatings, inhibitors, and multi-alloying to alleviate the corrosion of steel. Multi-alloying is an effective method to reduce destructive corrosion [1,2,3,4]. Morcillo et al. [5] used a polarizing microscope to study the rust layer of weathering steel and found that the dark gray in the optically homogeneous inner rust layer is composed of iron oxides of some amorphous substances, while the optically active outer rust layer is yellow or red. It is composed of some FeOOH, and the amorphous α-FeOOH is promoted by alloying elements such as Cu, Cr, P, etc., which can effectively hinder the entry of oxygen and corrosive ions. In the past, traditional bridge steel was often manufactured through the addition of a large number of Cu, Cr, Ni, and other alloying elements to improve the corrosion resistance of the steel itself, increasing production costs. Adding an appropriate amount of rare earth elements into steel instead of some precious metal elements can improve the corrosion resistance of steel, such as enhancing its self-corrosion potential and reducing galvanic corrosion without decreasing the mechanical properties, thereby reducing the cost of production inputs, which has been confirmed by many scholars [6,7,8,9,10,11]. Lian et al. [12] studied the corrosion mechanism of carbon steel and rare earth low-alloy steel under the conditions of dry–wet cycle immersion and pointed out that rare earth elements have a micro-alloying effect. Rare earth elements are concentrated near the steel matrix of the inner rust layer, which promotes the enrichment of alloying elements in the rust layer near the matrix. RE atoms will preferentially enrich in pores and defects at the interface, segregate between the rust layer and the matrix, and form a RE segregation zone. The formation of the RE atomic layer is beneficial in accelerating the healing of defects and blocking the channel between the corrosive medium and the substrate. Gong Weiwei [13] conducted an in-depth study of the rust layer of rare earth Q420 high-strength steel after neutral salt spray immersion by Raman spectroscopy. The results revealed that the corrosion products initially contained a loose Fe2O3 structure, which then transformed into a dense structure consisting of Fe3O4 and α-FeOOH, following the reduction of rust and the subsequent re-oxidation of the obtained reductants. During atmospheric corrosion, steel initially exists in the form of FeOH+ and Fe2+ ions at the initial stage of atmospheric corrosion, and is then subjected to air oxidation, generating γ-FeOOH; later on, with the enhancement of the acidity of the humid film or electrolyte solution, γ-FeOOH dissolves and gradually generates amorphous hydroxyl iron oxides or α-FeOOH [14]. At the same time, research by Zhang TY. [15] shows that the addition of rare earth elements inhibits the cathodic activation process, including the hydrogen evolution reaction and oxygen reduction. After adding rare earth elements, the self-corrosion potential of the steel is positively shifted to enhance its self-corrosion potential, which confirms the positive effect of rare earth elements on reducing the dissolution of the steel. However, most of these results are based on laboratory studies with high levels of added rare earth elements (>40 ppm). The addition of rare earth elements can improve the corrosion resistance of steel, but it tends to form large particles of inclusions in the liquid steel, which can reduce the impact toughness of the steel and even block the pouring risers, affecting the production efficiency. To prevent large rare earth inclusions in the continuous casting process from blocking the pouring riser, rare earth elements are usually added in trace amounts (<30 ppm). In recent years, many researchers have proved that the addition of ultra-micro rare earth Ce in steel can refine the grain size of the original austenite and also improve the transformation of the small-angle grain boundary and the Σ3 grain boundary, thus reducing the grain boundary’s energy and effectively hindering the occurrence of pitting corrosion [16,17].
In this study, three groups of self-designed Q370qENH steel with micro-rare earth content were systematically studied by simulating an indoor corrosion test and an outdoor exposure corrosion test, and the corrosion behavior and protection of Q370qENH steel in the rust layer in a marine atmosphere were revealed. The effects of Ce on the distribution of α-FeOOH and micro-area element distribution in the Q370qENH steel were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and FE-EPMA. The corrosion products produced by the corrosion of the experimental steel were analyzed using the X-ray diffraction (XRD) technique to evaluate the effect of Ce micro-alloying on the ‘phase’ protection in the rust layer. The corrosion resistance of experimental steel with different Ce content was studied using a three-electrode electrochemical workstation. A theoretical reference basis is provided for the industrialized production of rare earth steel under the conditions of a continuous casting process.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Experimental Materials

In this study, the experimental steel Q370qENH was designed according to the national standard GB/T 714-2015 [18], both by melting in a 210 t converter with top and bottom re-blowing and refining in an LF (ladle furnace) + RH (Ruhrstahl Heraeus) furnace. It consists mainly of dehydrogenation gas and carbon and is cast into 220 mm thick slabs in a continuous casting machine, then rolled into a test steel plate with a thickness of 20 mm using the TMCP (thermo-mechanical control process). The rolling process parameters are as follows.
The roughing opening temperature was maintained at 1180 ± 15 °C, the finishing opening temperature at 910 ± 15 °C, the final rolling temperature at 810 ± 15 °C, and the final cooling temperature at 650 ± 15 °C with a cooling rate of 10 °C/s. The rare earth element (Ce) was added as a micro-alloying element during the smelting process of the micro-alloyed steel. Metallographic samples were cut using the wire cutting method, ground on 80–2000 mesh sandpaper, and corroded with a 4% nitric acid alcohol solution to surface ash. The microstructures of the three test steels are shown in Figure 1 and are composed of polygonal ferrite and strip black pearlite.

According to the GB/T 714-2015 standard for bridge steel, the mechanical property samples were obtained using the prescribed sampling method. Under the requirements of GB/T 228.1-2010 [19], three types of steel plates were cut using wire cutting to obtain tensile test steel samples. The tensile tests were conducted using a WDW-200 universal material testing machine (Bairuo instrument, Shanghai, China) at a speed of 2 mm/min and a temperature of 25 °C. 2.2. Experimental Methods

Preparation of salt spray test samples: A molybdenum wire cutting machine was used to cut along the rolling direction of the experimental steel, and the samples were processed into two sizes of 30 mm × 25 mm × 5 mm and 15 mm × 15 mm × 5 mm. Each group of parallel samples was three pieces, and the cut samples were polished smooth with sandpaper. The sample was used to calculate the corrosion rate and for SEM observation of surface morphology, and the small-sized sample was used to measure the elemental analysis of the micro-region of the EPMA cross-section and electrochemical experiments. Three groups of specimen steel, 1#, 2#, and 3#, were placed in the LRHS-270-RY-type salt spray box. According to the GB-T 10125-2012 [20] neutral salt spray test requirements, the salt spray test temperature will be set at 25 °C, the corrosion solution in the salt spray box is a 3.5% NaCl corrosion solution, and the corrosion acceleration test will be set for 3 d, 9 d, 16 d, 24 d, and 28 d cycles. After each cycle of corrosion, the specimen was macroscopically photographed with a digital camera. The surface rust layer of the specimen is scraped off with a piece of wood and saved for testing and analysis.
The atmospheric exposure test was conducted in Laoshan District, Qingdao. During the exposure process, regular sampling was performed to characterize the rust layer and record temperature, humidity, and weather conditions. The test followed the the GB/T 14165-2008 standard [21], using aluminum brackets and frames made of hanging sheet exposure racks. High-strength weather-resistant bridge steel plates were wire-cut into sizes of 100 mm × 50 mm × 5 mm. The cut experimental steels, labeled as 1#, 2#, and 3#, were punched with 2 holes, 1 hole, and 0 holes, respectively, with a hole diameter of Φ2 mm. The specimens were polished until a uniform smooth surface was achieved. Three parallel samples were taken from each group to calculate the corrosion rate, observe the microstructure, and analyze corrosion products.
The rusty samples were soaked in a specific solution containing 500 mL hydrochloric acid, 500 mL distilled water, and 4.5 g hexamethyltetramine. The samples were soaked until they showed a metallic luster, then weighed and recorded using an electronic balance. After rust removal, the average corrosion rate was calculated using the weight loss method ASTM G 1-03 [22], as shown in Formula (1).

The powdered rust layer was analyzed for phase composition after corrosion. FE-EPMA was used for cross-section observation of the rust layer and micro-area elemental analysis. German-Brugger-Bruker D8 ADVANCE multifunctional XRD (Bruker AXS, Beijing, China) was used to test the angular range of 10~80 °C under a Cu target at a tagged voltage of 40 KV and a current of 30 mA.
Corrosion electrochemical tests were conducted using a three-electrode system. The sample to be tested with rust served as the working electrode, a Pt electrode as the auxiliary electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode as the reference electrode. The frequency range of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was 10−2 Hz–105 Hz, and the amplitude of the applied perturbation AC was 10 mv. The test time was 1000 s, and the test was carried out after the open-circuit voltage had stabilized. The electrochemical impedance spectra and polarization curves were recorded after achieving a stable state.

3. Results

3.1. Mechanism of Rare Earth Element Ce on Inclusions

The SEM and EBSD patterns of 0RE and 10 ppm RE experimental steels are shown in Figure 2. It can be observed that the 0RE experimental steel is mainly long-strip manganese sulfide, and most of the Mn S is above 7 μm under the electron microscope. In addition, the corrosion pit is due to the dissolution of MnS inclusions around the matrix, and the oxygen concentration difference is formed inside and outside. In order to maintain electrical neutrality, the concentration of Cl− in the corrosion pit increases, resulting in further corrosion of the steel substrate. Cao et al. [23] calculated the work function of the crystal plane of inclusions in steel by first principles. Comparing the ability of non-metallic inclusions and rare earth composite inclusions to induce pitting corrosion, it is found that the work function of manganese sulfide inclusions is much lower than that of the steel matrix. The size of the work function represents the strength of the bound electrons in the material, and the lower energy required for the electrons to escape reflects the corrosion tendency of the material itself.
Figure 2. Morphology (left) and energy spectrum (right) of inclusions in 0RE and 10 ppm RE experimental steels.
The 10 ppm RE experimental steel is mainly composed of elliptical rare earth composite inclusions smaller than 3 μm, and manganese sulfide inclusions are basically not observed under the scanning electron microscope. This indicates that the rare earth element Ce can change the morphology and size of non-metallic inclusions in steel. Through the energy spectrum, it is found that the content of O and Ce elements in the bright white part of the outer ring of rare earth composite inclusions is high, and the middle gray black area mainly contains S, Al, and other elements, which can effectively slow down the corrosion of the contact area with the matrix and further protect the steel matrix. Due to the NaCl corrosion solution, the Ce2O3-Ce2O2S inclusions in contact with the steel matrix will preferentially dissolve and act as an anode. Until the reaction of rare earth composite inclusions is completed, the electrochemical activity of the corrosion site decreases, and the corrosion of the steel matrix tends to be uniform, as shown below:
Through subsequent comparative experiments, it was found that there were large or deep corrosion pits on the surface of the 0RE experimental steel, which was due to the poor thermal stability of non-metallic inclusions such as sulfides, its strong ability to induce pitting corrosion, and corrosion that is easily able to destroy the steel matrix. Niu, G et al. [24] used SKPFM to analyze the potential difference relationship between rare earth inclusions and the matrix. Compared to the steel without rare earth elements, the surface potential distribution of the experimental steel with rare earth elements is more uniform, which reduces the corrosion tendency in the corrosion pit. On the other hand, the thermal expansion coefficient of rare earth inclusions in steel is similar to that of the steel matrix. Therefore, in the process of steel rolling or machining, non-metallic inclusions such as Mn S should not be elongated and crushed along the rolling direction, which can lead to the concentration of stress, destroy the continuity of the steel matrix, and endanger the corrosion resistance of steel.

3.2. Simulated Marine Atmospheric Environment

3.2.1. Corrosion Rate

The corrosion rates obtained in the salt spray experiment test are shown in Figure 3. The average corrosion rate of the three groups of experimental steel can be divided into three stages. The first stage is the rapid increase in the corrosion rate (0~9 d). In the early stage of corrosion, the steel substrate is exposed to air, forming a loose rust layer that lacks protective properties. The second stage is the rapid decline stage of the corrosion rate (9~24 d). With the passage of time, the surface of the steel substrate is completely covered by the rust layer, and the gradual densification of the rust layer hinders the penetration of corrosive ions in the atmosphere. The corrosion rate of the experimental steel containing rare earth elements decreases faster than that of the experimental steel without rare earth elements. In the third stage (24~28 d), the corrosion rate tends to be gentle. Compared with the 0RE experimental steel, the corrosion rate of experimental steel containing rare earth Ce is the lowest. It shows that the addition of ultra-micro rare earth elements in steel can effectively reduce the corrosion rate. In addition, with the increase in the additional amount, the corrosion rate will gradually decrease.
Figure 3. The average corrosion rate of experimental steels with different Ce contents in 3.5% NaCl solution.

3.2.2. Corrosion Macroscopic Morphology

Figure 4 shows the macroscopic morphology of the experimental steel. With the extension of the corrosion cycle, the color of the rust layer changed from reddish brown to orange-yellow and dark brown, with a small amount of black corrosion products [25]. Part of the corrosion products on the surface of the steel substrate also gradually changed from the unstable phase to the stable phase. From the graph, there are some exposed parts on the surface of the three experimental steels, indicating that they are not completely corroded. As the corrosion cycle progresses, by day 9, the corrosion products have predominantly and uniformly covered the surfaces of the experimental steels, and the corrosion rate begins to exhibit a turning point. Among the experimental steels, the rust layer on the surface of the 1# steel without added rare earth elements appears relatively loose, possibly due to the scouring effect of wet cycles during the salt spray process, which makes the loose rust layer more prone to detachment. In contrast, the experimental steels containing rare earth elements (2# and 3#) demonstrate a faster transformation of the rust layer into stable phases. With the increase in corrosion time, compared with three different test steels containing Ce over the same period, a small number of dark brown areas appeared on the surface of the test steel in the later stage of corrosion, and the dark brown rust layer basically had no protective ability. When the corrosion cycle reached 28 d, the rust layer color of the test steel containing 10 ppm is more uniform and the rust layer is flat.
Figure 4. Macroscopic morphology of experimental steel containing rare earth elements over different corrosion periods. (a) Macroscopic morphology of 1# experimental steel without rare earth elements; (b) Macroscopic morphology of 2# experimental steel containing 6 ppm of rare earth elements; (c) Macroscopic morphology of 3# experimental steel containing 10 ppm of rare earth elements.
Figure 5 shows the macroscopic morphology of the experimental steel 1#, 2#, and 3# after rust removal at each period. During the mechanical descaling process of the experimental steel for each corrosion cycle, the initially formed rust layer is thin and easily removable. As the corrosion progresses, the rust layer becomes thicker and stratified. Compared with the experimental steel containing rare earth elements, the outer rust layer on the sample without rare earth elements exhibits poor adhesion, being loose and easy to remove. However, the inner rust layer near the steel substrate is firmly adhered and extremely difficult to remove. This indicates that the inner rust layer becomes denser with prolonged corrosion time. After removing the rust, the surface of the steel matrix displays uneven pits. The severity of these pits becomes more pronounced with longer corrosion cycles. After day 9 of the corrosion cycle, the surface of sample 1# shows a more obvious corrosion line depth compared to samples 2# and 3#. This demonstrates that the surface rust layer of the experimental steel containing rare earth elements is more compact, effectively hindering the immersion of corrosive Cl− ions with a small radius and alleviating further corrosion of the steel matrix. Moreover, under the influence of harmful inclusions in steel, the steel matrix continues to corrode inside the matrix in the form of pitting corrosion. As the corrosion time passes, individual deep and significant corrosion pits gather earlier, and the diameter of corrosion pits gradually expands [6]. The uneven distribution of corrosion pits leads to different degrees of corrosion in various parts of the steel matrix, which is detrimental to performance. On the surface of the experimental steel with added rare earth elements, there are no individual deep corrosion pits, and the corrosion degree of the entire matrix is more uniform. In summary, the addition of rare earth elements to steel benefits the uniform corrosion of the steel substrate’s surface, resulting in the formation of a dense rust layer and hindering the immersion of corrosive ions.
Figure 5. Macroscopic morphology of experimental steel containing rare earth elements after rust removal under different corrosion periods. (a) Macroscopic morphology of 1# experimental steel without rare earth elements after de-rusting; (b) Macroscopic morphology of 2# experimental steel containing 6 ppm of rare earth elements after de-rusting; (c) Macroscopic morphology of 3# experimental steel containing 10 ppm of rare earth elements after de-rusting.

3.2.3. Micro-Morphology of Corrosion Products

In order to further investigate the influence of rare earth elements on the microscopic morphology of corrosion products in high-strength weather-resistant bridge steel, the surface corrosion product morphology of the experimental steel was observed after a corrosion cycle of 9 days. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used for this purpose, and the results are presented in Figure 6. The three groups of experimental steel corrosion product layers are mainly composed of large plate-like β-FeOOH, needle-like or petal-like γ-FeOOH, and spherical α-FeOOH [26]. The rust layer structure of experimental steel 1# is mainly composed of β-FeOOH and γ-FeOOH structural phases, and the surface of the rust layer of experimental steel 3# is mainly composed of round α-FeOOH. Numerous studies have demonstrated that during the initial phases of corrosion, a significant amount of needle-like γ-FeOOH will be produced. Simultaneously, in the Cl−-rich solution, iron atoms due to the oxidation of FeCl2 or FeCl3 hydrolysis produce large flakes of β-FeOOH because of the instability of both γ-FeOOH and β-FeOOH itself, which makes it easy for other corrosion products to transform [27,28,29,30]. The rare earth elements facilitate the ongoing electrochemical reaction of the corrosion products on the surface of the steel substrate, resulting in the formation of a more stable phase. As a result, the morphology of the rust layer of the experimental steel with 10 ppm of rare earth elements mainly appears as spherical α-FeOOH.
Figure 6. The effect of rare earth elements on the microstructure of test steel in simulated marine atmospheric corrosion for 9 days.
It can be seen from the figure that a continuous particle rust layer was formed on the surface of the three groups of experimental steel. There are obvious holes and cracks in the corrosion products on the surface of the experimental steel without rare earth elements, which provides a channel for oxygen and corrosive media such as water to enter, so it is consistent with the increase in the above corrosion rate. There are different shapes and sizes of corrosion voids on the surface of the steel substrate, indicating that the corrosion of the substrate surface is uneven and the degree of corrosion is uneven, which is not conducive to the improvement of the overall performance of the steel. Compared with the experimental steel without rare earth elements, the pores of the rust layer on the surface of the experimental steel with 6 ppm of RE are reduced. The rust layer of the experimental steel with 10 ppm of RE has no large pores. In summary, the addition of trace amounts of rare earth elements to steel has a positive impact on reducing porosity and preventing anion erosion, thereby enhancing the corrosion resistance and overall performance of the steel.

3.2.4. Cross-Section Morphology and Element Distribution of the Rust Layer of Q370qENH Steel with Different Ce Contents

Based on the previous findings that the experimental steel with 10 ppm of RE has the best corrosion resistance, further analysis was conducted to verify the role of rare earth elements in improving corrosion resistance in the steel. Figure 7 shows the electron probe scanning and energy spectra of the cross-section of the rust layer after 28 days. In the case of the experimental steel 3#, some surface rust layer detachment and cracking occurred during embedding. From the figure, the color changes in the energy spectra indicate the distribution of certain elements in the rust layer. The thickness of the alloying elements enriched in the cross-section rust layer of the experimental steel can be divided into the inner rust layer. The thickness of the enriched alloying elements in the inner rust layer of the 1# and 3# experimental steels is 29 μm and 50 μm, respectively. It was observed that there is no significant enrichment of Cl elements in the enriched alloying elements. Comparing the experimental steel with added rare earth elements to high-strength weathering bridge steel without rare earth elements, it was found that the thickness of the alloying elements enriched in the rust layer of the experimental steel was increased by 42%. The hydroxyl oxides of Fe in the rust layer coordinate with Cr3+ to form α-CrxFe1-xOOH, which exhibits anodic selectivity [31]. This is beneficial in preventing the intrusion of corrosive ions. It can be concluded that the addition of rare earth elements in the steel can regulate the role of Cu and Cr alloying elements, inhibit the corrosion rate, promote the formation of a dense rust layer, and improve the corrosion resistance of the steel.
Figure 7. Effect of rare earth elements on the structure of the rust layer after 28 days of corrosion in experimental steel, as measured by the EPMA spectrum.

3.2.5. Analysis of Corrosion Products

An XRD analysis of corrosion products was conducted on the three groups of experimental steels for a duration of 9 days, with the results presented in Figure 8. The main corrosion products observed in the three experimental steels were found to be similar. The crystalline composition of the rust layer consisted of Fe3O4, α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH, a small quantity of β-FeOOH, and amorphous aggregates. The color and chemical properties of iron oxides and hydroxyl oxides provided insights into the rust layer. The color of Fe3O4 is black, which is the conductive phase in the rust layer phase. It can be used as an electron channel for the corrosion reaction to make the corrosion reaction continue. During the corrosion process, the rust layer’s structure is in a dynamic transition and is not in a stable state. However, the Fe3O4 phase has relative chemical stability, so it can be regarded as a protective corrosion product. FeOOH, on the other hand, displayed a yellowish-brown color. It comprised unstable γ-FeOOH and β-FeOOH, along with the more stable α-FeOOH. The structure of β-FeOOH is loose and porous, and most of the anions, such as Cl−, 0H-, etc., pass through the rust layer to the steel substrate to provide a channel to accelerate corrosion. The higher the Gibbs free energy of the corrosion product, the more thermodynamically unstable it is and the more likely it is to occur, but it is easily converted into other products, namely α-FeOOH < γ-FeOOH < β-FeOOH [32,33,34]. During the corrosion process, it spontaneously transforms to α-FeOOH, which can be considered the most stable compound among the three rust phases.

The experimental steel with extended corrosion time exhibits a rust layer that gradually separates into internal and external rust layers. The external rust layer is called γ-FeOOH, and β-FeOOH is easily converted to α-FeOOH. Additionally, the dehydration phenomenon contributes to the production of the Fe3O4 reaction.
In the initial corrosion cycle test of experimental steel, it was observed that corrosion products gradually accumulated on the surface of the steel matrix. The presence of rare earth elements in the steel promoted the formation of alloying elements as a nucleus core in the inner rust layer. This led to the development of dense and stable amorphous aggregates, effectively isolating anions and preventing oxygen intrusion. As a result, the corrosion rate of the steel matrix started to slow down. To evaluate the protective factor of the rust layer, the ratio of α-FeOOH to γ-FeOOH content, denoted as α/γ, was used as a corrosion standard. A higher α/γ value indicated a lower corrosion rate, but this standard was only applicable in industrial atmospheres. In marine atmospheric environments, the rust layer contained a higher proportion of α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH, and oxide Fe3O4, while Cl− ions formed complexes with Fe3+ ions, enhancing the stability of Fe3+ and facilitating the generation of β-FeOOH and its crystal growth process. It was discovered that the selectivity of hydroxyl oxides and Fe oxides followed the order: Fe3O4 < α-FeOOH < γ-FeOOH < β-FeOOH [35]. Consequently, a new rust layer protective factor, denoted as α/γ*, was proposed, where γ* comprised β-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH, and Fe3O4, and α represented α-FeOOH. The semi-quantitative analysis of corrosion products from the three experimental steel groups is presented in Table 3. From the table, it can be observed that the α/γ* values for experimental steel 1#, 2#, and 3# were 0.26, 0.353, and 0.414, respectively. These results indicate that a higher rare earth content in the steel led to greater α/γ* values, suggesting that rare earth elements promote the transition of unstable corrosion products to stable amorphous aggregates and the phase transition of α-FeOOH. These findings align with previous experiments regarding the corrosion-resistant mechanism.

3.3. Analysis of the Properties of Corrosion Products in Outdoor Exposure Experiments

3.3.1. Macroscopic Morphology of Outdoor Exposure Corrosion

Three groups of experimental steel were selected in order to carry out natural environment atmospheric exposure tests in the Laoshan District of Qingdao from September to December 2022. Meteorological conditions during the exposure period are outlined in Table 4. Table 5 presents the corrosion rates of the experimental steel. The corrosion rates for the three sets of experimental steel exposed to air were 0.0752 mm/a, 0.0669 mm/a, and 0.0665 mm/a. The corrosion rates of experimental steel 1# were higher than those of experimental steel 3# and 2#, at 11.6% and 11%, respectively. This suggests that the addition of trace amounts of rare earth elements to bridge steel can effectively enhance its resistance to corrosion in marine atmospheres. Furthermore, as the amount of rare earth elements in the steel increases, its resistance to corrosion from these elements also increases. Therefore, the laboratory’s simulated accelerated corrosion test confirms the feasibility of the real environment.

Figure 9 illustrates the macroscopic morphology of the rust layer on the experimental steel. It can be observed that the surface of the experimental steel is predominantly covered with corrosion products, appearing as orange-brown rust layer particles. Visual inspection reveals that the color of the rust layer does not significantly differ among the three experimental steel groups. This observation aligns with the macroscopic morphology of bridge steels with varying cerium (Ce) contents during the initial corrosion period in the simulated marine environment mentioned earlier. Therefore, it further confirms that the rare earth element content does not have a substantial effect on the macroscopic color of the rust layer in the initial corrosion period under a specific corrosion environment.
Figure 9. The macro-morphology of the rust layer of test steel with different rare earth content levels exposed to the outdoor atmosphere for 3 months.
Figure 10 shows the macroscopic morphology of the experimental steel after de-rusting. Upon magnification, it is evident that all three specimens (1#, 2#, and 3#) exhibit a significant number of corrosion pits on their surfaces. The corrosion behavior in marine atmospheric conditions is known for its complexity and diversity and is influenced by various factors. Research has indicated that the impact of atmospheric corrosion is primarily determined by three environmental variables: wetting time, the SO2 content in the atmosphere, and salt particle content. Considering the proximity of the Laoshan District in Qingdao to the Yellow Sea, the presence of water vapor and rainfall containing aggressive sea salt particles accelerates the corrosion of the specimens. Consequently, all three groups of experimental steel demonstrated severe corrosion. Notably, experimental steel 3#, which contains rare earth elements, displayed a partially uncorroded surface after descaling, with smaller corrosion pits present. This can be attributed to the inclusion of long-strip manganese sulfides in the steel, which are influenced by the metamorphic effect of rare earth elements, which reduces the source of corrosion and slows down the corrosion rate of the steel substrate.
Figure 10. Effect of rare earth elements on the macroscopic morphology of the samples after descaling.

3.3.2. Outdoor Exposure Corrosion Micro-Morphology

Figure 11 shows the microscopic morphology of the rust on the sample surfaces under SEM. The microstructure of the rust layer of experimental steel 1# without rare earth elements has bubbles and microcracks, as well as signs of shedding. The overall shape is not flat and it is easily washed by rain and shedding. Aggressive Cl− ions, water molecules, and even dust impurities can easily diffuse through the pores and cracks of the rust layer into the substrate, etc. The shape of the rust layer of experimental steel 2# is comparatively better, while the surface of experimental steel 3# is relatively flat, and it appears to be more compact. Rare earth elements can promote the enrichment of alloying elements and change the structure and microstructure of the rust layer, and the final protective rust layer is different, so the corrosion rate of experimental steel 3# is the lowest. Comprehensive results show that the addition of trace rare earth elements in bridge steel promotes the transformation of the rust layer to the stable corrosion product α-FeOOH, which is conducive to the formation of a dense rust layer and can effectively impede the process of metal dissolution and oxygen reduction and improve corrosion resistance.
Figure 11. Effect of rare earth elements on the microscopic morphology of rust in experimental steels exposed to the outdoor atmosphere.

3.3.3. Corrosion Products

Figure 12 illustrates the XRD patterns of the rust layer formed on the experimental steel. The XRD patterns indicate that the main corrosion products within the rust layer are α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH, and Fe3O4. It is worth noting that the β-FeOOH phase is not present in the rust layer of all three groups of experimental steels. This absence may be due to the relatively shorter atmospheric exposure time and the low concentration of Cl− ions in the air, which is below the detection limit of XRD.

Based on the table, it can be concluded that the content of α-FeOOH is lower in the high-strength weathering bridge steel without added rare earth elements compared to the high-strength weathering bridge steel with added rare earth elements. Furthermore, the stability of α-FeOOH content increases with the increase in rare earth content. These findings are consistent with the results obtained from experiments in a laboratory-simulated marine atmosphere with accelerated corrosion. It should be noted that the accumulation of corrosion products takes a considerable amount of time in outdoor exposure conditions, and only the relative content of each corrosion product differs. 3.4.1. Polarization Curve

The open-circuit potentials of three groups of corroded experimental steels after 9 days in a simulated marine atmospheric corrosion environment are shown in Figure 13. Among them, the experimental steel without rare earth elements showed the lowest open-circuit potential, while the experimental steel with 10 ppm RE showed the highest open-circuit potential. The experimental steel containing 6 ppm RE is between the two, with the second-highest open-circuit potential. In addition, it can be observed that the open-circuit potential tends to be stable over time and reaches a stable value.
Figure 13. Effect of rare earth elements on the open-circuit potential diagram of samples.
The electrochemical polarization test of the experimental steel can be concluded as shown in Figure 14. From the diagram, it can be seen that the cathodic corrosion process of the rusted sample after 9 days is basically similar, but the anodic polarization curve is different. The anodic corrosion current of the test steel containing 10 ppm is significantly reduced, indicating that the rust layer formed during the corrosion process has the property of inhibiting anodic dissolution. At the same time, with the increase in rare earth element content, the self-corrosion potential of the test steel gradually shifts in the positive direction, and the self-corrosion potential of the test steel containing 10 ppm has the largest positive shift. In other words, the addition of rare earth elements reduces the electrochemical activity of the steel substrate surface and is an important factor in improving the corrosion resistance of rare earth steel undergoing long-term comprehensive corrosion. The self-corrosion potential represents the degree of sensitivity of the material to corrosion. The more positive the self-corrosion potential is, the lower the corrosion tendency is, so the corrosion resistance of materials can be judged by its self-corrosion potential. The Tafel fitting data of the three groups of experimental steels are shown in Table 7. From the table, compared with the 1# experimental steel without rare earth elements, the self-corrosion potential of experimental steels 2# and 3# bearing rare earth elements increased by 4.6% and 19%, respectively. The addition of rare earth elements obviously reduces the corrosion current density of the steel, and the corrosion inhibition tendency of this experimental steel is shown as 1# < 2# < 3#. This indicates that the increase in the content of rare earth elements added to the steel hinders the charge transfer between the electric double layer and reduces corrosion.

3.4.2. Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy

Figure 15 and Figure 16 show the Nyquist diagram and Bode diagram of the three groups of test steel under simulated marine atmospheric environment corrosion for 9 days.
Figure 15. Effect of rare earth elements on the Nyquist diagram of experimental steels.
Figure 16. Effect of rare earth elements on the Bode diagram of experimental steel.
Figure 15 is the electrochemical impedance behavior of the experimental steel in the same concentration of NaCl solution. There is only one capacitive arc in the high-frequency region of the electrochemical behavior of the experimental steel. The capacitive arc in the high-frequency region reflects the electric double layer’s structure, composed of the rust layer and the solution, and the radius of the capacitive arc reflects the degree of obstruction of charge transfer. Consequently, the larger the arc radius of the material electrochemical test, the better the corrosion resistance of the steel [36]. The experimental steel without rare earth elements has the smallest arc resistance radius, and the arc resistance span gradually becomes larger with the increase in rare earth content, which indicates that the experimental steel’s rust layer is gradually densifying and thickening, so that it inhibits the electrochemical reaction taking place. It can be concluded that the ability of the three kinds of experimental steel to reduce the corrosion rate can be ranked as 3# > 2# > 1#, which is in line with the above three groups of corrosion rate data of the experimental steel. The Bode diagram indicates the corrosive medium-blocking capability of the rust layer, which reflects the value of the impedance in the electrochemical reaction process. The Bode diagram shows that the three experimental steels have the same trend for the change in the modulus value of the impedance. However, the modulus value of sample No. 3 is the largest, followed by sample No. 2, and sample No. 1’s value is the smallest. Because the modulus value reflects the ability of the rust layer to protect the substrate, the corrosion resistance of test steel 3# is the best. Combined with the results of SEM and XRD, it can be concluded that the addition of rare earth elements can promote the increase of α-FeOOH content in the rust layer, contribute to the role of the diffusion barrier, block the electrochemical channel, and increase the impedance value. At the same time, the transformation of rare earth oxide composite inclusions and the decrease in Cl− adsorption trends promote the reduction of surface electrochemical activity and inhibit the corrosion rate.
Zview was used to fit the simulated equivalent circuit diagram, as shown in Figure 17. Table 8 shows the fitted values of the simulated equivalent circuits for the three groups of experimental steels. Rs represents the solution resistance, which is related to the concentration of the components of the solution and can be ignored due to the same corrosive medium. Rct is the charge transfer resistance, which reflects the resistance to electron migration. n represents the dispersion factor, and the value of n is related to the roughness of the electrode and the uniformity of the current distribution. The range of the n value is between 0–1, and the larger the n value is, the smoother the electrode surface is and the more uniform the surface current distribution is (Table 8). The Rct values of experimental steels 3# and 2# are 47.4% and 31.7% higher than that of experimental steel 1#, respectively. Combined with the electrochemical impedance spectra and polarization curves, it shows that the addition of rare earth elements to high-strength weathering bridge steel effectively improves corrosion resistance, and the atmospheric corrosion resistance of high-strength bridge steel is enhanced with the increase in cerium content. Thereby, the bridge steel with 10 ppm RE has the best corrosion resistance.

4. Conclusions

(1) Under the mutual verification of simulated marine atmospheric and outdoor atmospheric exposure corrosion experiments, the addition of trace levels of cerium improves the seawater corrosion resistance of Q370qENH bridge steel, thereby reducing the rate of corrosion and enhancing the protection ability of the rust layer.
(2) The rare earth element promotes the enrichment of alloying elements in the inner rust layer to form a dense structure, which induces the transformation of the outer rust layer into the inner rust layer, hinders the anode reaction, and improves corrosion resistance. Secondly, Ce induces the transformation of the unstable-phase γ-FeOOH to the stable-phase α-FeOOH, reduces the crack gap of the rust layer, and inhibits the penetration of the corrosive ion Cl- into the steel matrix, thereby enhancing the protection of the rust layer.
(3) By adding rare earth elements to steel, non-metallic inclusions such as long-strip Mn S can be modified into elliptical rare earth composite inclusions, which reduces the size of the inclusions. It also changed the electrochemical corrosion of inclusions in the micro area, so that the surface corrosion of the experimental steel tended to be uniform. At the same time, the self-corrosion potential of the test steel was increased, the surface current tended to be uniform, the corrosion current density was reduced, and the corrosion rate was inhibited.
