## Introduction

Reinforced concrete is vital for infrastructure projects like bridges, docks, tunnels, and subways, owing to its excellent cost-performance ratio and mechanical strength. It offers exceptional resistance to various environmental stresses, including abrasion, chemical attacks, and fatigue. However, its long-term durability, critical for serviceability, is compromised by combined deterioration mechanisms such as chloride-induced corrosion, carbonation, alkali-silica reactions, and freeze-thaw cycles[1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR1 "Fu, C., Fang, D., Ye, H., Huang, L. & Wang, J. Bond degradation of non-uniformly corroded steel rebars in concrete. Eng. Struct. 226, 111392 (2021)."),[2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR2 "Hornbostel, K., Angst, U. M., Elsener, B., Larsen, C. K. & Geiker, M. R. Influence of mortar resistivity on the rate-limiting step of chloride-induced macro-cell corrosion of reinforcing steel. Corros. Sci. 110, 46–56 (2016)."),[3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR3 "Zhao, Y., Dong, J., Ding, H. & Jin, W. Shape of corrosion-induced cracks in recycled aggregate concrete. Corros. Sci. 98, 310–317 (2015)."). Among these, steel reinforcement corrosion in highly aggressive environments—particularly marine settings with high salinity, humidity, and wet-dry cycles—remains a persistent challenge despite extensive research and mitigation efforts[4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR4 "Mundra, S., Criado, M., Bernal, S. A. & Provis, J. L. Chloride-induced corrosion of steel rebars in simulated pore solutions of alkali-activated concretes. Cem. Concr. Res. 100, 385–397 (2017)."),[5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR5 "Berrocal, C. G., Lundgren, K. & Löfgren, I. Corrosion of steel bars embedded in fibre reinforced concrete under chloride attack: state of the art. Cem. Concr. Res. 80, 69–85 (2016)."),[6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR6 "Tang, S. W., Yao, Y., Andrade, C. & Li, Z. Recent durability studies on concrete structure. Cem. Concr. Res. 78, 143–154 (2015).").

In marine environments, chloride ingress represents the predominant degradation mechanism, accounting for over 60% of reinforced concrete failures[7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR7 "Tran, D. T., Lee, H.-S., Singh, J. K. & Lee, D.-E. Corrosion prevention of steel rebar embedded in the cement mortar under accelerated conditions: combined effects of phosphate and chloride ions. Constr. Build. Mater. 365, 130042 (2023)."). Chloride ions—either intrinsic to construction materials or externally derived from seawater—diffuse through the concrete matrix via capillary action and permeation, ultimately accumulating at the steel-concrete interface[8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR8 "Xiong, C. et al. Preparation of phytic acid conversion coating and corrosion protection performances for steel in chlorinated simulated concrete pore solution. Corros. Sci. 139, 275–288 (2018)."),[9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR9 "Liu, Q. et al. A novel green reinforcement corrosion inhibitor extracted from waste Platanus acerifolia leaves. Constr. Build. Mater. 260, 119695 (2020)."). While numerous conventional approaches have been investigated to mitigate chloride attack—including enhancing concrete density[10](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR10 "Moffatt, E. G., Thomas, M. D. & Fahim, A. Performance of high-volume fly ash concrete in marine environment. Cem. Concr. Res. 102, 127–135 (2017)."), increasing cover thickness, applying protective coatings[11](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR11 "Aguirre-Guerrero, A. M., Robayo-Salazar, R. A. & de Gutiérrez, R. M. A novel geopolymer application: coatings to protect reinforced concrete against corrosion. Appl. Clay Sci. 135, 437–446 (2017)."), employing electrochemical techniques[12](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR12 "Calero, J. C., Llorca, M. C. & Terradillos, P. G. Influence of different ways of chloride contamination on the efficiency of cathodic protection applied on structural reinforced concrete elements. J. Electroanal. Chem. 793, 8–17 (2017)."), and modifying reinforcement surface characteristics[13](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR13 "Etteyeb, N., Dhouibi, L., Takenouti, H. & Triki, E. Protection of reinforcement steel corrosion by phenylphosphonic acid pre-treatment PART II: tests in mortar medium. Cem. Concr. Compos. 65, 94–100 (2016)."),[14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR14 "Etteyeb, N., Dhouibi, L., Takenouti, H. & Triki, E. Protection of reinforcement steel corrosion by phenyl phosphonic acid pre-treatment PART I: tests in solutions simulating the electrolyte in the pores of fresh concrete. Cem. Concr. Compos. 55, 241–249 (2015).")—these strategies fundamentally modify the steel’s external environment rather than its intrinsic corrosion resistance. This critical limitation inherently constrains their long-term protective effectiveness and durability, particularly in harsh marine conditions. A more fundamental solution lies in alloy design and microstructural optimization of the steel itself[15](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR15 "Liu, M., Cheng, X., Li, X. & Lu, T. J. Corrosion behavior of low-Cr steel rebars in alkaline solutions with different pH in the presence of chlorides. J. Electroanal. Chem. 803, 40–50 (2017)."),[16](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR16 "Roventi, G., Bellezze, T., Giuliani, G. & Conti, C. Corrosion resistance of galvanized steel reinforcements in carbonated concrete: Effect of wet–dry cycles in tap water and in chloride solution on the passivating layer. Cem. Concr. Res. 65, 76–84 (2014)."),[17](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR17 "Du, F. et al. Chloride ions migration and induced reinforcement corrosion in concrete with cracks: a comparative study of current acceleration and natural marine exposure. Constr. Build. Mater. 263, 120099 (2020)."). By enhancing the material’s inherent corrosion resistance through compositional and microstructural engineering, this approach offers a more sustainable and potentially more effective strategy for combating chloride-induced corrosion in marine concrete structures, addressing the root cause rather than merely mitigating symptoms.

Recent studies[18](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR18 "Ida, N., Nishimoto, M., Muto, I. & Sugawara, Y. Role of MnS in the intergranular corrosion and depassivation of sensitized Type 304 stainless steel. npj Mater. Degrad. 8, 2 (2024)."),[19](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR19 "Kuah, K. X. & Blackwood, D. J. Investigating molybdenum’s sulphur scavenging ability for MoS2 formation in preventing pitting corrosion of stainless steels. npj Mater. Degrad. 7, 80 (2023)."),[20](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR20 "Li, Z. et al. Role of segregation behavior of Cu and Sb in the region of inclusions on initial corrosion. npj Mater. Degrad. 7, 29 (2023)."),[21](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR21 "Liu, P., Zhang, Q.-H., Watanabe, Y., Shoji, T. & Cao, F.-H. A critical review of the recent advances in inclusion-triggered localized corrosion in steel. npj Mater. Degrad. 6, 81 (2022)."),[22](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR22 "Saito, H., Nishimoto, M. & Muto, I. Pitting corrosion characteristics of sintered Type 316 L stainless steel: relationship between pores and MnS. npj Mater. Degrad. 8, 61 (2024).") have provided detailed insight into how MnS inclusions initiate localized corrosion in carbon and stainless steels. MnS tends to dissolve preferentially, creating micro-galvanic cells between the inclusion and surrounding matrix and triggering early pitting. These works collectively highlight that inclusion chemistry and the local electrochemical environment govern pit initiation and propagation. Rare earth (RE) elements serve as potent alloying additives that enhance steel purity by refining non-metallic inclusions, thereby improving the pitting corrosion resistance of rebars. Beyond inclusion control, RE elements promote uniform matrix dissolution and participate in corrosion product formation, facilitating a denser, more protective rust layer. Chromium (Cr), on the other hand, is a well-known corrosion-resistant element that plays a critical role in forming a stable passive oxide layer on steel surfaces. This passive layer acts as a protective barrier, preventing the penetration of corrosive agents, particularly in chloride-contaminated environments. Cr also enhances the steel’s resistance to localized corrosion such as pitting, which is a common issue in concrete reinforcement exposed to aggressive chloride ions. Although both RE and Cr individually enhance the corrosion resistance of rebars, their synergistic effects have not been fully explored. The interaction between RE and Cr may result in more complex and effective corrosion protection mechanisms, offering superior performance compared to using either element alone. Addressing this knowledge gap is crucial for developing advanced corrosion-resistant rebar materials, especially in harsh environments with high chloride concentrations.

This study systematically investigates Cr/RE-regulated inclusion effects on microstructural evolution, electrochemical behavior, and passive film properties through comprehensive electrochemical testing and immersion experiments in chloride-containing SCP solutions. By elucidating the fundamental mechanisms of Cr/RE-enhanced corrosion resistance, this work provides critical insights for designing durable reinforcement materials for marine applications.

## Results

### Microstructure characterization

Figure [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig1) presents the metallographic structures of the three steel rebar specimens, revealing consistent microstructural features dominated by ferrite-pearlite constituents with minor granular bainite, all exhibiting characteristic banded morphology. Chromium’s strong ferrite-stabilizing effect significantly reduced pearlite content, while rare earth additions modified carbon activity in ferrite, increasing its carbon solubility and consequently altering the ferrite/pearlite ratio.

**Fig. 1: Micrometallurgical structure of three samples.**

**a** HRB400; **b** HRB400-Cr; **c** HRB400-Cr-RE.

Figure [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig2) shows the morphological and compositional evolution of inclusions in HRB400 steel following Cr and RE additions. Untreated HRB400 and Cr-modified samples contained irregular MnS and Al₂O₃ inclusions along with (Ca,Mg,Al)-O composites (Fig. [2a, b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig2)). These inclusions exhibited angular geometries (spherical, reticular, or elongated) with sharp edges and sizes <2 μm—features known to initiate microcracks and degrade both mechanical properties and pitting resistance[23](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR23 "Reformatskaya, I., Rodionova, I., Beilin, Y. A., Nisel’Son, L. & Podobaev, A. The effect of nonmetal inclusions and microstructure on local corrosion of carbon and low-alloyed steels. Prot. Met. 40, 447–452 (2004)."),[24](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR24 "Szklarska-Smialowska, Z. Influence of sulfide inclusions on the pitting corrosion of steels. Corrosion 28, 388–396 (1972)."),[25](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR25 "Domizzi, G., Anteri, G. & Ovejero-Garcıa, J. Influence of sulphur content and inclusion distribution on the hydrogen induced blister cracking in pressure vessel and pipeline steels. Corros. Sci. 43, 325–339 (2001)."). The types of inclusions in HRB400-Cr-RE steel bar with RE addition are REAlO 3 and REAlO 3 inclusions wrapped by MnS, and the single MnS inclusions disappear.

**Fig. 2: SEM/EDS results of inclusions across the three steels.**

**a** Al₂O₃–MnS clusters together with Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO phases in HRB400; **b** HRB400-Cr is (Ca, Mg, Al)–O complex oxides in HRB400-Cr; **c** RE–Al–O enveloped by thin MnS shells in HRB400-Cr-RE.

To quantify the effect of alloying on inclusion characteristics, a statistical analysis of inclusion size distribution was conducted based on more than 100 inclusions per sample. As shown in Fig. [S1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#MOESM1), Cr and RE additions markedly refined the inclusions: the fraction of inclusions smaller than 2 µm increased sharply, while that of inclusions larger than 5 µm decreased. This refinement contributes to a more uniform inclusion dispersion and improved interfacial stability, which are beneficial for corrosion resistance.

### Potentiodynamic polarization curves

Figure [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig3) presents the potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) curves of the three reinforcing steels in saturated Ca(OH)₂ simulated concrete pore solution (SCP) with increasing NaCl concentrations, while Table [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Tab1) compiles the corresponding pitting potentials (E pit) and passive current densities (i pass). Irrespective of alloy type, the anodic branches in Fig. [3a–c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig3) enter the passive region directly, without a discernible active–passive transition, and the virtually superimposed cathodic branches indicate that oxygen reduction and hydrogen evolution jointly govern the cathodic kinetics. The congruent profiles imply that the underlying corrosion mechanism remains unchanged despite the Cr/RE alloying; only the kinetics are altered.

In SCP containing 0.5%, 1%, and 2% Cl⁻, all electrodes anodically passivate, whereas the cathodic reaction remains charge-transfer controlled. The quantitative data in Table [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Tab1) reveal a pronounced alloying benefit. At low chloride concentrations (0.5 wt.%), the passive films formed on both steels remain stable, and the corrosion process is mainly controlled by the passive film’s breakdown potential rather than by inclusion activity. Under these mild conditions, the Cr–RE effect is less pronounced. At 1 wt.% Cl⁻ (Fig. [3b](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig3)), E pit rises from 292 mV for HRB400 to 446 mV for HRB400-Cr and 654 mV for HRB400-Cr-RE, while i pass falls concomitantly. Similar trends persist at 2 wt.% Cl⁻ (Fig. [3c](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig3)); HRB400-Cr-RE exhibits the lowest i pass (11.3 µA cm⁻²), one-third that of HRB400 (38 µA cm⁻²). At 3.5 wt% Cl⁻ (Fig. [3d](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig3)), the curves lose the sharp passivation jump, displaying instead a sluggish active-to-passive transition that signals severe attack on the passive film. Even under this aggressive condition, HRB400-Cr-RE retains the highest E pit and the lowest i pass (Table [1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Tab1)), underscoring the potent synergy of Cr and RE in suppressing chloride-induced corrosion.

### Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy

Figure [4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig4) presents the impedance spectra (including Nyquist and Bode) for the three rebar samples exposed to chloride-containing SCP. All Nyquist traces show single, slightly depressed capacitive arcs, confirming that Cr/RE micro-alloying modifies only the kinetics while preserving the interfacial mechanism. The arc radii follow the order HRB400 < HRB400-Cr < HRB400-Cr-RE, indicating a progressive improvement in charge-transfer resistance due to the influence of Cr and RE.

**Fig. 4: EIS spectra of in SCP solution containing different concentrations of sodium chloride.**

**a**~**d** Nyquist diagram, **e**~**h** Bode diagram, **a**, **e** 0.5 wt% NaCl; **b**, **f** 1 wt% NaCl; **c**, **g** 2 wt% NaCl; **d**, **h** 3.5 wt% NaCl. **i** Equivalent circuits used to model the EIS spectra.

Although the Bode plots show a single dominant time constant, a weak high-frequency shoulder in the Nyquist plots indicates the contribution of a surface film. Hence, a two-time-constant equivalent circuit was employed to describe both the film and charge-transfer processes. HRB400-Cr-RE attains phase angles close to 80°, whereas HRB400 barely reaches 70°, and the impedance modulus follows the same trend. Figure [4b–d](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig4) reveal that elevating Cl⁻ from 1 wt% to 3.5 wt% shrinks the arcs, yet the ranking HRB400-Cr-RE > HRB400-Cr > HRB400 remains intact. Consequently, Cr/RE alloying systematically enlarges interfacial resistance and markedly improves chloride tolerance.

Impedance spectra were analysed with ZSimpWin by fitting the equivalent circuit shown in Fig. [4i](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig4); the model describes all interfacial physicochemical processes and yields excellent agreement with the experimental data (χ² < 0.01), in line with earlier work[26](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR26 "Liu, M., Cheng, X., Li, X., Jin, Z. & Liu, H. Corrosion behavior of Cr modified HRB400 steel rebar in simulated concrete pore solution. Constr. Build. Mater. 93, 884–890 (2015)."). The extracted parameters in Table [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Tab2) are defined as follows: R s reflects the ohmic resistance of the bulk solution; R f and Q f describe the barrier properties and non-ideal capacitance of the surface film; R ct and Q dl quantify the electron-transfer resistance and the double-layer response at the metal–electrolyte interface. A constant-phase element was substituted for an ideal capacitor to accommodate the distributed capacitance arising from surface heterogeneity, as outlined in Eq. ([1](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Equ1)):

$${Z}_{CPE}=\frac{1}{{Y}_{0}{(j\omega )}^{n}}$$

(1)

In the expression, _Y_ ₀ denotes the CPE constant, _ω_ the angular frequency, _j_ ² = –1, and _n_ the dispersion parameter. A value of _n_ = 1 signifies ideal capacitive behaviour, _n_ = 0 indicates pure resistance, and _n_ = –1 corresponds to inductive response, whereas 0 <_n_< 1 points to interfacial heterogeneity.

The R s values were verified after extending the frequency range to 100 kHz and were found to be stable. The constant-phase element Q f was converted to an effective film capacitance C f using the Brug equation. The dispersion coefficient indicates a mixed capacitive–diffusive response associated with ionic transport through the Ce/La-enriched passive film. Table [2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Tab2) reveals that introducing Cr and RE raises R ct from 7.8 kΩ cm 2 in plain HRB400 to 11.6 kΩ cm 2 in HRB400-Cr-RE under 0.5 wt% Cl⁻–SCP, indicating a more compact and Cr/RE-enriched surface layer that outperforms conventional iron oxides in retarding charge transfer[27](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR27 "Liu, M., Cheng, X. & Li, X. Corrosion propagation behavior of low-Cr steel Rebars in simulated concrete environments. Int. J. Electrochem. Sci. 14, 726–742 (2019)."). Furthermore, the overall corrosion resistance can be gauged by the polarization resistance, Rp, defined here as the sum of R ct and Rf[28](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR28 "Yuan, X., Wang, X., Cao, Y. & Yang, H. Natural passivation behavior and its influence on chloride-induced corrosion resistance of stainless steel in simulated concrete pore solution. J. Mater. Res. Technol. 9, 12378–12390 (2020)."). Mirroring the Nyquist trends, the polarization resistance (R p) consistently ranks HRB400-Cr-RE > HRB400-Cr > HRB400 at every chloride level, underscoring that Cr/RE incorporation suppresses interfacial charge transfer and strengthens long-term durability[29](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR29 "Yan, L., Song, G.-L., Wang, Z. & Zheng, D. Crevice corrosion of steel rebar in chloride-contaminated concrete. Constr. Build. Mater. 296, 123587 (2021)."). Importantly, HRB400-Cr-RE preserves the highest Rp even at elevated chloride concentrations, a trend that mirrors the superior pitting resistance observed in the potentiodynamic curves of Fig. [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig3).

### Mott-Schottky curves

The semiconducting nature of the passive films can be rationalised by molecular-orbital theory. The space-charge capacitance C (F cm⁻²) is related to the applied potential E (V) and the flat-band potential E fb (V) by the Mott–Schottky (M-S) relation in Eq. ([2](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Equ2)):

$$\frac{1}{{C}^{2}}=\pm \frac{2}{\varepsilon {\varepsilon }_{0}eN}\left(E-{E}_{fb}-\frac{kT}{e}\right)$$

(2)

Alternatively, the thickness of the space charge layer (L) can be calculated using Eq. ([3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Equ3)):

$$L=\frac{2\varepsilon {\varepsilon }_{0}}{e{N}_{D}}\sqrt{\left(E-{E}_{fb}-\frac{kT}{e}\right)}$$

(3)

Where the capacitance of the surface oxide, C (F cm⁻²), scales with the applied bias _E_ (V) and the flat-band potential _E_ _fb_ (V), the latter extracted from the x-intercept of the linear Mott–Schottky trace. Its dielectric response is expressed through the vacuum permittivity (_ε₀_) and the film’s relative permittivity _ε_ at 298 K, while thermal and electronic contributions are captured by the Boltzmann constant (_k_), absolute temperature T, and elementary charge (e = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C). Positive slopes in the Mott–Schottky plot denote n-type behaviour; negative slopes signify p-type conduction.

Figure [5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig5) presents the Mott–Schottky curves for the three steels in SCP with increasing NaCl. All films exhibit a linear, positive-slope region between −0.5 and +0.35 V (vs SCE), characteristic of n-type semiconductors[30](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR30 "Liu, M., Cheng, X., Li, X., Pan, Y. & Li, J. Effect of Cr on the passive film formation mechanism of steel rebar in saturated calcium hydroxide solution. Appl. Surf. Sci. 389, 1182–1191 (2016)."). The donor densities derived from these slopes are plotted in Fig. [5e](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig5). Elevated N D values point to non-stoichiometric, highly defective oxide lattices[31](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR31 "Xu, L. et al. Structural characteristics and chloride intrusion mechanism of passive film. Corros. Sci. 207, 110563 (2022)."). Irrespective of chloride concentration, HRB400-Cr-RE consistently registers the lowest N D; for example, at 1 wt% NaCl, N D falls from 6.19 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for HRB400 to 2.68 × 10¹⁹ cm⁻³ for HRB400-Cr-RE. Based on Eq. [4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Equ4), Cr/RE alloying thickens the space-charge layer and thereby suppresses ingress of aggressive species, explaining the markedly higher corrosion resistance observed in both EIS and potentiodynamic tests.

### Corrosion behavior in Cl-containing simulated concrete pore fluids

Figure [6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig6) summarises the corrosion rates after immersion for three days. HRB400 exhibited the highest initial rate (0.12 mm/a), whereas both Cr- and Cr/RE-modified steels registered markedly lower values. Over this period, the Cr/RE alloy reduced the corrosion rate by ~67% relative to conventional HRB400, reflecting the rapid establishment of a dense, Cr/RE-rich oxide barrier[32](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR32 "Liu, M. et al. Indoor accelerated corrosion test and marine field test of corrosion-resistant low-alloy steel rebars. Constr. Build. Mater. 5, 87–99 (2016)."). This finding aligns with the potentiodynamic, EIS, and Mott–Schottky data. As immersion continued, the rates for all steels declined progressively as additional corrosion products accumulated and further stabilised the surface[33](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR33 "Yang, D., Yan, C., Zhang, J., Liu, S. & Li, J. Chloride threshold value and initial corrosion time of steel bars in concrete exposed to saline soil environments. Constr. Build. Mater. 267, 120979 (2021).").

### Corrosion behavior in early stage

Early-stage corrosion initiation was examined by immersing the three rebar grades in saturated Ca(OH)₂ + 3.5 wt% NaCl for 10 and 30 min, followed by FE-SEM/EDS analysis. Figures [7](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig7), [8](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig8) display the FE-SEM micrographs and corresponding EDS analyses of inclusions in the three rebar specimens both prior to and following immersion in 3.5 wt.% NaCl-containing saturated Ca(OH)₂ solution for 10 and 30 minutes, respectively.

**Fig. 7: SEM and EDS of inclusions before and after 10-minute immersion in SCP solution containing 3.5 wt% NaCl.**

**a**–**a1** HRB400. **b**–**b1** HRB400-Cr. **c**–**c1** HRB400-Cr-RE.

**Fig. 8: SEM and EDS of inclusions before and after 30 min immersion in SCP solution containing 3.5 wt% NaCl.**

**a**–**a1** HRB400, **b**–**b1** HRB400-Cr, **c**–**c1** HRB400-Cr-RE.

HRB400 (Fig. [7a₁](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig7)) contains irregular Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO complex inclusions. After 10 min these inclusions begin to dissolve, opening micro-gaps at the inclusion/matrix interface and triggering localized pitting. Density-functional calculations of work functions (experimentally corroborated[34](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR34 "Zhang, X. et al. Effects of niobium and rare earth elements on microstructure and initial marine corrosion behavior of low-alloy steels. Appl. Surf. Sci. 475, 83–93 (2019).")) establish the corrosion-propensity sequence RE–Al–O–S > Fe > Al₂O₃; the less-noble Fe matrix is therefore preferentially dissolved adjacent to Al₂O₃-rich particles, progressively widening the interface gap.

In HRB400-Cr-RE (Fig. [7c₁](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig7)), MnS inclusions dissolve completely within 10 min, whereas RE–Al–O–S particles dissolve only partially. The higher electrochemical potential of RE–Al–O relative to MnS drives this selective dissolution, generating micropits that nucleate preferentially at inclusion corners and edges. Matrix attack is minimal at this stage, confirming that early corrosion is governed by inclusion chemistry rather than by the steel matrix itself.

After 30 min of exposure (Fig. [8a₁](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig8)), HRB400 showed extensive matrix dissolution around the Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO inclusions, producing large cavity-type pits surrounded by thick corrosion products. Two synergistic effects drive this acceleration: (i) the interfacial gap that forms as the inclusion dissolves[34](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR34 "Zhang, X. et al. Effects of niobium and rare earth elements on microstructure and initial marine corrosion behavior of low-alloy steels. Appl. Surf. Sci. 475, 83–93 (2019).") and (ii) lattice distortion arising from the thermal-expansion mismatch between the inclusion and the Fe matrix[35](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#ref-CR35 "Wei, J., Dong, J., Ke, W. & He, X. Influence of inclusions on early corrosion development of ultra-low carbon bainitic steel in NaCl solution. Corrosion 71, 1467–1480 (2015)."). Both factors promote rapid Fe dissolution and pit nucleation.

In HRB400-Cr, the (Ca,Mg,Al)-O complex inclusions remain essentially intact after 30 min (Fig. [8b₁](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig8)). Their anodic character and limited chloride adsorption preclude dissolution or detachment, so pitting continues to be governed by the earlier MnS dissolution rather than by these oxide particles. HRB400-Cr-RE contains RE–Al–O particles initially encapsulated by MnS layers (Fig. [8c₁](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig8)). After 30 min the MnS shell is completely removed and the RE–Al–O cores begin to dissolve; some inclusions have vanished entirely, leaving stable pits. Comparison of Fig. [8b₂](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig8) (30 min) with earlier 10-min micrographs confirms that both pit depth and corrosion-product coverage increase markedly with immersion time.

The results demonstrate that inclusions act as preferential corrosion initiation sites, with localized matrix dissolution occurring around MnS or Al₂O₃ particles where surface potential is lower. Once pits nucleate, accumulated corrosion products form occluded cells that accelerate propagation. In contrast, the (Ca,Mg,Al)–O complex inclusions in HRB400-Cr remain intact after 30 min immersion—neither dissolving nor detaching—thereby suppressing pit initiation. This stability underpins the superior corrosion resistance of HRB400-Cr, a conclusion corroborated by both potentiodynamic and EIS data. In HRB400-Cr-RE, early attack originates from RE–Al–O–MnS and RE–Al–O inclusions. Rare-earth addition markedly lowers sulfur content, eliminating discrete MnS; The residual RE-rich inclusions—whether RE–Al–O or MnS-sheathed RE–Al–O—act as robust barriers that suppress pit propagation, substantially enhancing the global corrosion performance of HRB400-Cr-RE.

### Observation of corrosion morphology after immersion

Figure [9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig9) presents a comprehensive comparison of the corrosion morphologies exhibited by the three rebar grades after 3, 7 and 15 days of exposure to chloride-contaminated simulated concrete pore solution. During the initial three-day period, HRB400-Cr-RE maintained an almost uniform surface with only sparse, shallow pits, signifying minimal corrosive attack, whereas HRB400-Cr already displayed distinct chloride-induced pits highlighted by yellow arrows, and HRB400 exhibited pronounced rust accumulation. This morphological hierarchy remained consistent with the weight-loss trends shown in Fig. [6](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Fig6), corroborating the superior chloride resistance of HRB400-Cr-RE. As immersion was extended to 7 and 15 days, HRB400 underwent a progressive transition from localized pitting to generalized corrosion, accompanied by a marked increase in both the volume and particle size of corrosion products and a corresponding decline in structural stability. In contrast, HRB400-Cr exhibited moderate product build-up with enlarging yet still discrete pits, while HRB400-Cr-RE retained a thin, adherent oxide layer that intermittently exposed the underlying substrate without excessive scaling. The sustained protection afforded to HRB400-Cr-RE is attributed to the stabilizing effect of Cr/RE micro-alloying, which promotes a dense, self-healing passive film and effectively suppresses excessive corrosion-product formation throughout the entire exposure period.

These inclusion-controlled mechanisms are fully consistent with the electrochemical data. HRB400-Cr-RE exhibits the most positive pitting potential and the lowest passive current density, the largest Nyquist arc radii, the lowest donor density, the thinnest corrosion-product layer and smallest pits after 3 d, and the shallowest pit depths and lowest aspect ratios. CLSM confirms that RE–Al–O–S inclusions—whether free-standing or MnS-encapsulated—are more effective at suppressing early corrosion than single-phase MnS, Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO or (Ca,Mg,Al)-O inclusions.

In summary, rare-earth inclusions introduced by Cr/RE micro-alloying preserve the integrity of the passive film during early exposure, maintaining a benign pH > 12 environment and yielding markedly higher passive-film resistance. Over extended periods or at high chloride concentrations, gradual inclusion dissolution and detachment erode this protection, but HRB400-Cr-RE still outperforms conventional grades because the RE-rich phases delay both pit initiation and propagation.

This study investigates the corrosion behavior of Cr/RE-modified low-alloy steel rebars in chloride-contaminated simulated concrete pore solutions. In HRB400 steel, MnS and Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO inclusions are transformed into RE–Al–O–S particles with RE addition, and corrosion initiates with the dissolution of MnS, exposing the RE–Al–O core and forming stable pits. RE-modified inclusions significantly delay pit initiation, with HRB400-Cr-RE showing the highest pitting potential, lowest passive current density, and largest Nyquist arc radius, along with the lowest corrosion rate and shallowest pits. Corrosion resistance follows the order HRB400-Cr-RE > HRB400-Cr > HRB400. As chloride concentration increases, pitting potential decreases, passive current density rises, capacitive arc radii shrink, and donor density increases, reflecting the progressive dissolution of inclusions and a corresponding decrease in overall corrosion resistance.

## Methods

### Materials and solution

Three low-alloy rebars samples are obtained from Xinxing Ductile Iron Pipe Co., Ltd in China. After compositional verification in Table [3](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Tab3) by Optical emission spectrometer (OES), the alloys underwent hot-rolling processing. The rare earth elements incorporated in this study are cerium (Ce) and lanthanum (La). For the introduction of these RE elements, a La-Ce alloy (60 wt% La) was employed. For metallographic analysis, we prepared 10 × 10 × 3 mm³ specimens from rolled plates, etched them in 4 vol.% nital solution for 5 seconds, then immediately rinsed with ethanol and dried with compressed air prior to microstructural characterization.

### Analysis and characterization of inclusions

For microstructural analysis, 10 × 10 × 10 mm³ steel samples were sequentially ground (400–4000 grit SiC paper) and mechanically polished, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in anhydrous ethanol and air drying. We performed comprehensive characterization using a JEOL JSM-7100F FE-SEM/EDS system at 30 kV, 10 nA probe current, and 10 mm working distance, analyzing ≥20 inclusions per sample for statistical reliability. Quantitative inclusion assessment (size distribution, area fraction) was conducted using an ASPEX automated analyzer to ensure rigorous microstructural evaluation.

### Electrochemical measurements

Electrochemical measurements were performed using a conventional three-electrode system, with the steel reinforcement specimens (10 × 10 × 5 mm 3) serving as the working electrode (1 cm 2 exposed area). A platinum sheet and saturated calomel electrode (SCE) were employed as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. Prior to testing, the working electrodes were encapsulated in epoxy resin to ensure geometric consistency, then sequentially ground to 2000-grit using silicon carbide abrasive paper. To eliminate surface contaminants, the specimens were ultrasonically cleaned in anhydrous ethanol and deionized water, followed by drying under controlled nitrogen flow to prevent oxidation.

The electrochemical tests were conducted in a simulated concrete pore solution (saturated Ca(OH)₂, pH ~12.5) at 25 ± 1 °C. The chloride concentration was systematically varied (0–3.5 wt% NaCl) to evaluate corrosion behavior under different aggressive conditions. Open-circuit potential (OCP) monitoring was first performed until stabilization (±2 mV/h variation), followed by potentiodynamic polarization tests. The polarization scans were initiated at -250 mV vs. OCP (cathodic direction) and terminated at an anodic current density of 1 mA/cm², with a scan rate of 0.33 mV/s.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were subsequently carried out at the stabilized OCP over a frequency range of 100 kHz to 10 mHz after immersion for 0.5 h, applying a 10 mV sinusoidal perturbation. The acquired impedance spectra were analyzed using ZsimpWin software, fitting the data to appropriate equivalent circuit models to extract key electrochemical parameters.

### Immersion test

Cylindrical steel samples (Ø20-22 mm × 50 ± 2 mm) were prepared by abrasive cleaning and precisely weighed (±0.0001 g). Accelerated corrosion tests were performed in chloride-contaminated simulated concrete pore solution (3-15 days). Post-immersion, corrosion products were removed using a standardized derusting solution (1:1 HCl:H 2 O with hexamethylenetetramine inhibitor) via 1-minute ultrasonic cleaning, followed by ethanol rinsing and drying. Corrosion rates were calculated from mass loss measurements using Eq. ([14](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41529-026-00746-3#Equ14)):

$${V}_{{corr}}=\frac{87600\times \Delta w}{S\times T\times \rho }$$

(14)

Δw denotes the mass change (g) of the coupon after immersion, S its exposed geometric area (cm²), T the exposure duration (h), and ρ the steel density (g cm⁻³).

Corrosion morphology was examined using FE-SEM on gold-sputtered specimens (10 × 10 × 3 mm³). 3D surface topography of derusted samples was quantified by Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM). XPS analysis (Kratos AXIS Ultra DLD) with Al Kα radiation characterized passivation films. Survey (1 eV step) and high-resolution (0.1 eV step) scans were energy-referenced to C 1 s (285.0 eV). Quantitative composition analysis was performed via spectral deconvolution (Avantage software) using standard reference spectra.
