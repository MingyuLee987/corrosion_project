## Abstract

17–7 PH stainless steel is a highly versatile material with a multitude of applications in a diverse range of fields, including aerospace, chemistry and petrochemistry, and medicine. The material’s exceptional mechanical properties and corrosion resistance render it the optimal selection for numerous components and instruments. Nevertheless, the surface properties of 17–7 PH stainless steel are inadequate for applications requiring high hardness and wear resistance in certain extreme environments. Due to its excellent mechanical properties and corrosion resistance, it can be utilized in the manufacturing of pharmaceutical equipment components. However, certain specialized environments still require surface nitriding treatment. Considering the complex heat treatment process required for this material, this paper reports a detailed study of the surface performance changes of 17–7 PH steel before and after ion nitriding following aging heat treatment. The study employs rolled 17–7 PH stainless steel as the subject material. The impact of heat treatment on plasma nitriding of stainless steel is investigated by comparing and analyzing the influence of martensite content and dislocation density within the martensite of the material prior to and following heat treatment on the hardness, thickness, and corrosion resistance of the nitrided layer on the surface of the steel after nitriding. The results demonstrate that 17–7 PH stainless steel, which does not undergo heat treatment, exhibits a high internal dislocation density, a high nitriding efficiency, and consequently, a high surface hardness. Following the application of a heat treatment, there is an increase in the martensite content of 17–7 PH stainless steel, a decrease in the dislocation content, and an increase in the matrix hardness.

## 1. Introduction

Precipitation-hardened stainless steels exhibit excellent corrosion resistance, ductility, and high strength [1]. Among these, 17–7 PH is the most common semi-austenitic precipitation-hardened stainless steel. It possesses favorable mechanical properties, corrosion resistance, and adaptability to heat treatment, which collectively contribute to its extensive utilization in diverse applications, including railroad structures, aerospace industry components, and power plant components [2,3,4,5]. The imparting of high strength and toughness is possible with solution and aging treatments. However, the low hardness of the material limits its application in challenging designs requiring a combination of high strength, high toughness, and wear resistance, such as turbine blades, tools, bearings, and even orthopedic surgeries. To address this limitation, various surface modification techniques have been applied to improve these properties, including ion implantation [6], laser surface treatment [7], carburizing [8], carbonitriding [9], and nitriding [10], where the latter has proven to be the most popular. Nitriding is a surface-strengthening method that has been demonstrated to improve hardness and wear resistance [11,12,13,14,15]. Nevertheless, a significant proportion of the research has concentrated on the nitriding of single-phase stainless steels [16,17,18,19].

D. Wang et al. [20] conducted a detailed study on the microstructure evolution of 17–7 PH precipitation-hardened stainless steel during low-temperature gas nitriding. The results indicated that long-distance transport of metal atoms could occur under nitriding at lower temperatures, noting how interfaces promoting metal atom diffusion could effectively promote nitride precipitation. A spinodal decomposition of delta ferrite (δ-F) was observed in the process of low-temperature gas nitriding, likely caused by a high nitrogen concentration during nitriding leading to higher compression residual stress [21]. It was proposed that NiAl precipitates would form within blocky δ-ferrites [2]. Additionally, due to the presence of a martensite structure, many dislocations were found in precipitation-hardened stainless steel after aging treatment [22]. These dislocations serve to reduce the activation energy of N diffusion, thereby facilitating the precipitation of strengthening phases.

Despite the abundance of research on 17–7 PH nitriding, there remains a paucity of studies on the optimization of nitriding efficiency. With regard to nitriding efficiency, the principal influencing factors are temperature and diffusion activation energy. Sun [23,24,25,26] employed surface mechanical attrition treatment (SMAT) as a preliminary treatment prior to nitriding and observed that SMAT markedly increased the thickness of the nitrided layer. This was attributed to the enlargement of grain boundaries and a decrease in the activation energy of diffusion, which facilitated the diffusion of nitrogen atoms. Shen et al. [27] improved the nitriding efficiency and lowered the nitriding critical temperature by using a hybrid modification method of shot peening pretreatment and plasma nitriding. This was attributed to the fact that the increase in dislocations and grain refinement reduced the diffusion activation energy of nitrogen, thus facilitating the diffusion of nitrogen atoms. To enhance the efficacy of plasma nitriding of stainless steel, Lu et al. [28] subjected the steel to cold rolling, resulting in significant plastic deformation. Following the nitriding process, the thickness of the nitrided layer exhibited a notable increase, exceeding a twofold expansion.

Clearly, non-equilibrium defects such as grain boundaries and dislocations can significantly reduce the diffusion activation energy of nitrogen, leading to an increased nitriding efficiency. The rolled 17–7 PH stainless steel was found to possess a high density of dislocations and an appreciable degree of residual stress. Following heat treatment, a large and uniform martensite structure was obtained, which included a substructure of dislocations, albeit modified in terms of number and distribution. Moreover, the residual strain reduced to a certain extent after tempering. Therefore, significant changes were observed in the microstructure of 17–7 PH under different states, which is a key factor in the nitriding process. In this study, 17–7 PH precipitation-hardened stainless steel, subjected to hot rolling and subsequent heat treatment, was employed as a substrate prior to nitriding. The effects of microstructure, morphology, hardness, and corrosion resistance of the resulting nitrided layer were subjected to detailed analysis. This study analyzes and discusses the response of the material to nitriding with different microstructures. Following heat treatment, an increase in martensite content was observed in 17–7 PH stainless steel, accompanied by a reduction in dislocation content. The findings indicate that the nitriding treatment of the matrix and surface layer of 17–7 PH stainless steel results in the attainment of relatively high strength and hardness, rendering it an appropriate material for the manufacture of pharmaceutical equipment parts. Furthermore, an understanding of the properties of the material following nitriding in different heat treatment stages can provide guidance for the processing and assembly of parts.

## 2. Materials and Methods

### 2.1. Material

In this work, the material was 17–7 PH austenite–martensite precipitation-hardened stainless steel, and the chemical composition of the steel is reported in [Table 1](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-t001). The original specimen, designated S, was subjected to hot rolling and subsequently processed into a plate with dimensions of 15 mm × 15 mm × 8 mm using a wire EDM machine. The material was then mechanically ground to produce heat-treated and nitrided specimens with a smooth surface. The grinding process was as follows: (a) Rough grinding with sandpaper, involving the use of grit sizes 150#, 500#, 1000#, 1500#, and 2000#. (b) Fine polishing conducted using diamond grinding paste, with the grit size decreasing in increments of 3 μm from 9 μm (1800#) to 6 μm (3000#) to 3 μm (8000#).

### 2.2. Heat Treatment

Before heat treatment, the sample was ground and polished to remove the oxide skin and oil stains on the surface. The heat treatment was carried out on an industrial Muffle furnace, and the heat treatment cycle is shown in [Figure 1](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f001). The treatment included three steps: (1) Solution treatment (Ts) was carried out at 1040 °C for 30 min, followed by water quenching. (2) Conditioning (T c) at 760 °C, where the sample was held at this temperature for 1 h in order to complete the transformation of austenite to martensite, followed by (3) aging (T a) at 500 °C for 2 h and cooling in the air.

### 2.3. Plasma Nitriding Experiment

The heat-treated specimens, named H, are then subjected to a mechanical grinding process. The H and original S samples were put into plasma nitriding equipment concurrently to ensure that they were treated under the same nitriding conditions. The experiments were conducted in an LDMC−75 A ion glow nitriding furnace(Wuhan Ande Heat Treatment Equipment Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) with a pulse frequency of 1000 Hz and a voltage of 650 V in ammonia (NH3) at a pressure of 450–500 Pa, a temperature of 530 °C, a current of 20 A, and a ramping up rate of 100 °C/h for 8 h. Samples were identified as SN and HN, with the initial letters indicating the treatment method before plasma nitriding and the last letter indicating the treatment of nitriding.

### 2.4. Characterization

Optical microscopy (OM) was used to analyze the microstructure. S−3700 N field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM) equipped with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) was used to observe the cross-sectional morphology and to analyze the elemental distribution. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was studied with a DX−2700 X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.154 nm). The scanning angle (2θ) ranged from 30° to 90°, and the scanning rate was 0.08°/s. Thin foils (80 μm) prepared by a twin-jet polisher in a (90% CH 3 COOH + 10% HClO 4) solution were observed by a transmission electron microscope (TEM, 2100, Jeol, Tokyo, Japan) at 200 kV.

An HVS−1000 Vickers microhardness tester (Beijing Hongchangxin Technology Co., LTD, Beijing, China) was used to measure the hardness of the surface and cross-section along the depth direction of the sample under a 500 g load. The corrosion resistance properties of the nitriding layers were evaluated using an electrochemical testing technique in a 3.5% NaCl solution through measuring anodic polarization curves with a sweeping speed of 1 mV/s at room temperature.

## 3. Results and Discussions

### 3.1. Microstructure and Morphology of Stainless Steel after Heat Treatment

[Figure 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f002) illustrates the surface morphology of the original samples S and H. The surface morphology of the original samples is presented in [Figure 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f002). As can be observed in [Figure 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f002), both S and H are composed of α’-slab martensite, γ-austenite, and δ-irregularly shaped ferrite. Following the application of heat treatment to the stainless steel, the percentage of α’-slab martensite increases. [Figure 3](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f003) illustrates the thickness of the nitrided layers of the SN and HN samples after nitriding. [Table 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-t002) presents the thickness measurements of the nitrided layers at several locations.

[Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f004) illustrates the surface morphology and EDS results of the nitrided samples SN and HN following nitriding. As illustrated in [Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f004)a,c, the nitrided layers formed by SN and HN exhibit an evident double-layer structure [29]. Stainless steel has a faster growth rate of its highly nitrogen-expanded layer when the driving force is sufficient during the nitriding process [30]. In conjunction with the distribution of nitrogen elements depicted in [Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f004)b,d, the double-layer thicknesses of the nitrided layers are estimated to be 95 μm and 35 μm, 40 μm and 70 μm, respectively. The nitrided layer lacks the structural characteristics present in the original matrix and comprises an N-forming expanded solid solution. The outermost C layer contains CrN, nitrogen–martensite, and high-nitrogen-expanded austenite, and the composite layer is darker in color due to the large amount of CrN formed at the higher nitriding temperature. The bright white area of the S layer is the low-nitrogen zone, consisting of nitrogen-containing martensite and low-nitrogen-expanded austenite [29,30,31].

Furthermore, it can be observed from [Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f004) that the demarcation line between the nitrided layer and the substrate on the stainless steel surface was distinct and linear, while the thickness of the highly nitrogen-expanded austenite layer was diminished following heat treatment. Cracks were observed near the martensitic region of the nitrided layer and the matrix. This is attributed to the specimen surface undergoing an expansion during the nitriding process, leading to a discrepancy in lattice alignment between the matrix and the surface layer. The generation of a supersaturated solid solution of nitrogen in the lattice, the so-called “swelling” phase, leads to high compressive stresses and thus to the phenomenon of localized plastic deformation [32]. In addition, during the nitriding process, the concentration gradient of diffused nitrogen is too large, resulting in residual stresses in the nitrided layer, leading to cracks in the nitrided layer [33]. In particular, martensite, which is a body-centered cubic (BCC) structure with a low slip coefficient, is susceptible to cracking during nitriding.

Finally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was conducted to examine the dislocation density and diffusion of nitrogen in samples S and H prior to nitriding. [Figure 5](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f005)a,b,d,e illustrate that the S and H samples are primarily composed of lath martensite, exhibiting a substructure comprising a high density of entangled dislocations. These dislocations exhibit the organizational characteristics of low-carbon martensite. The dislocations in the S samples have a higher density and a larger distribution area. This is related to the strain applied during the rolling process. Moreover, the high-temperature tempering process resulted in a significant decrease in the dislocation density of the H samples, which was previously demonstrated in studies [34,35]. What is more, evidence suggests that the dislocations within sample S were not largely located at the laths of martensite like they were in the H sample, perhaps due to a relative absence of feature definition in the hot-rolled state (as seen in [Figure 2](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f002)a). These two forms of dislocation density and distribution might contribute to the differing capacities of nitrogen diffusion, resulting in different nitride production rates.

It is well known that the rate of elemental diffusion is influenced by two main factors: temperature and diffusion activation energy. [Figure 5](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f005)c,f illustrate that the diffusion channels of nitrogen in SN are greater than those in the HN sample, confirming the improvement of nitriding efficiency attributed to the lower diffusion activation energy of nitrogen brought about by non-equilibrium defects. The diffusion activation energy of the nitrogen atom at the grain boundary and dislocation is notably lower than that within the grain [26], at only 60%. Thus, dislocations provide more N diffusion channels and consequently improve diffusion efficiency, resulting in a thicker nitriding layer for the SN sample than the HN sample.

### 3.2. Physical Phase of Stainless Steel after Heat Treatment and Nitriding

[Figure 6](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f006) depicts the X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of the S, H, SN, and HN samples. Prior to nitriding, the S and H samples are primarily composed of α′-martensite and γ-austenite. Additionally, the diffraction peak shapes of both phases are more pronounced, and the intensity of the γ-austenite diffraction peaks is considerably lower than that of the α′-martensite diffraction peaks. This suggests that the fundamental phases of 17–7 PH stainless steel before and after heat treatment are predominantly martensitic, with some austenite present. Concurrently, the martensite strength in the H samples was markedly augmented following the heat treatment.

The SN samples at 2θ after nitriding exhibited two new diffraction peaks at 35.6° and 37.6°, which confirmed the presence of the CrN phase. However, the intensity of the diffraction peaks was weak, and the diffraction peaks exhibited broad characteristics, indicating that the CrN phase in the nitrided layer was fine and less abundant. After nitriding, the main in 17–7 PH stainless steel is still dominated by martensitic phase accompanied by some austenitic phase, maintaining the Bcc and Fcc structure. However, the peak shapes of both martensite and austenite diffraction peaks underwent a significant change, with the diffraction peaks broadening and the austenite peaks becoming significantly wider than the martensite. Additionally, the austenite peaks migrated to a lower angle. The octahedral gap radius of the γ lattice is larger than the tetrahedral gap radius of α′, resulting in an easier combination of nitrogen atoms with γ. This leads to an increase in the crystallographic spacing of γ, which in turn facilitates the formation of expanded austenite [17,36,37].

A comparison of the austenite diffraction peaks of the non-heat-treated stainless steel (SN) and the heat-treated stainless steel (HN) revealed that the diffraction peaks of SN were significantly broader than those of HN. This suggests that the expanded austenite content in SN is greater than that in HN.

### 3.3. Hardness of Stainless Steel after Heat Treatment and Nitriding

The surface hardness of the nitrided sample was tested under a load of 500 g, with the results shown in [Figure 7](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f007). It is evident that the modified layers of SN and HN samples reach 1487.34 HV 0.5 and 1401.72 HV 0.5, respectively, compared to 265.54 HV 0.5 and 338.78 HV 0.5 for the S and H samples, respectively. This is mainly due to the precipitation of precipitation-hardened phase after heat treatment, improving the surface hardness of the material. Apart from the residual compressive stress and deformation in the high nitrogen-rich layer [38], the lattice distortion caused by the solid solution of nitrogen atoms also contributes to the increase in hardness [39]. The analysis of [Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f004) and [Figure 6](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f006) substantiate this, as the SN sample features a thicker nitriding layer and a higher density of expanded austenite (γ N) and CrN hardened phase, thus explaining its higher surface hardness.

[Figure 8](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f008) illustrates the cross-sectional hardness of 17–7 PH before and after heat treatment and nitriding, as well as hardness indentation images of the nitrided layer–substrate junction. Following the application of a heat treatment, the cross-sectional hardness of the 17–7 PH matrix demonstrates a gradual increase due to the elevated martensite content and precipitation hardening, which is caused by the precipitation of NiAl nanoparticles from the 17–7 PH matrix [40,41]. Following nitriding, the decrease in the content of expanded austenite and expanded martensite with increasing section depth results in a gradual decrease in the section hardness of SN and HN with increasing section depth. In this instance, the cross-sectional hardness of HN exhibits a gradual decline with increasing section depth from 0 to 80 μm, followed by a pronounced acceleration in the rate of decline beyond this depth. Finally, the cross-sectional hardness at 140 μm is comparable to that of the substrate. In addition, the hardness of SN decreases more slowly at a cross-section depth of 0~100 μm; this is attributed to the higher dislocation density and superior nitriding efficiency of SN, which makes its nitrided layer cross-section hardness better than that of HN as a whole. Finally, the hardness of the nitrided layer is similar to that of the substrate at 160 μm.

In addition, in conjunction with the observed thicknesses of the nitrided layers of SN and HN, which are 130 μm and 110 μm, respectively, as depicted in [Figure 4](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f004). From the hardness indentation images, the hardness at the junction of the nitrided layer and the substrate is 670.69 HV0.5 and 641.76 HV0.5, respectively, and these values are much larger than the substrate hardness. This indicates that after nitriding, when the cross-section depth of the stainless steel exceeds the thickness of the nitrided layer, a certain amount of expanded martensite and expanded austenite still exists, but the amount is small and difficult to be observed.

### 3.4. Corrosion Behavior of Stainless Steel after Heat Treatment and Nitriding

The anodic polarization curves of S, H, SN, and HN samples in 3.5% NaCl solution are shown in [Figure 9](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-f009), from which the corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr) can be obtained as listed in [Table 3](https://www.mdpi.com/2075-4701/14/9/1061#metals-14-01061-t003). The corrosion current is proportional to the surface corrosion rate on the potential. Therefore, a larger corrosion current corresponds to a higher corrosion rate. Conversely, the corrosion potential is inversely proportional to the ease of corrosion of the material. Consequently, a lower corrosion potential indicates a greater ease of material corrosion. The 17–7 PH surface consists of three phases: ferrite, austenite, and martensite. During the corrosion process, ferrite is the initial phase to corrode due to its lowest corrosion resistance, resulting in a reduction of ferrite. Subsequently, the percentage of austenite and martensite compositions increases, resulting in a reduction in the corrosion rate. However, with the increase in anodic current density, the martensitic corrodes, resulting in an increase in the austenite percentage, which ultimately leads to the final S and H corrosion rates converging. This resulted in the typical localized solution passivation processes observed in the S and H samples. Furthermore, 17–7 ph stainless steel exhibits a reduction in corrosion resistance subsequent to heat treatment, which is attributed to an augmented martensite content. Consequently, the diminished corrosion potential is a consequence of this augmented martensite content.

Following nitriding, the corrosion properties of the SN and HN samples were reduced compared to both the S and H samples. The corrosion potential of the SN was greater than that of the HN, and the corrosion current density increased. Generally, γ N can effectively improve the corrosion resistance of stainless steel in corrosive media containing Cl−, because during the process of interaction between the γ N phase layer and the corrosive medium, the γ N phase layer releases nitrogen atoms, resulting in the formation of many NH4+ at the interface, which changes the pH value near the interface and increases the local pH value at the pit site, effectively reducing the acidity of pitting and inhibiting the self-catalytic action of Cl− [42,43]. However, when nitriding is carried out at higher temperatures, N binds tightly with Cr to form a stable nitrided chromium precipitation, resulting in poor corrosion resistance of the nitrided sample [16,27,44,45]. The generation of CrN on the surface of the stainless steel results in a reduction of Cr element contents in stainless steel, which is known as the Cr-poor phenomenon. This phenomenon leads to a reduction in the corrosion resistance of stainless steel. Since many dislocations in the S specimen provide a short path for nitrogen diffusion, which enables chromium atoms in the stainless steel to utilize this path for migration during nitriding, resulting in an increase in the CrN content of the SN specimen and a decrease in corrosion resistance.

## 4. Conclusions

Following the application of a heat treatment, the martensitic composition of 17–7 PH stainless steel undergoes an increase, which in turn leads to an enhancement in matrix hardness. However, this is accompanied by a reduction in dislocation density and corrosion resistance.

The surface layers of both SN and HN exhibited a bimodal structure, accompanied by an increase in the half-maximum full width of the martensite and austenite peaks following the nitriding treatment.

Following nitriding, the nitrided layer of HN exhibited a thickness of 140 μm, while the nitrided layer of SN reached 110 μm. However, the corrosion resistance was diminished in both cases due to the precipitation of CrN.
