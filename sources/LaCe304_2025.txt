Effects of La and Ce treatment on changes in inclusion characteristics
of 304 stainless steel during industrial production
Zhan-hua Shen1,2 • Lei Huang3 • Hua Zhang1,2 • Xue-zhong Huang3 • Cheng-song Liu1,2 • Yu-zhong Yang1,2 •
Hong-wei Ni1,2
Received: 8 October 2024 / Revised: 25 April 2025 / Accepted: 28 April 2025 / Published online: 26 August 2025
/C211 China Iron and Steel Research Institute Group Co., Ltd. 2025
Abstract
The extent of inclusion modiﬁcation achieved after adding various contents of rare earth elements to 304 stainless steel in
practical applications was explored, additionally the pitting behavior of the inclusions in a chloride environment before and
after modiﬁcation. The mechanisms by which the compositions of inclusions evolved throughout the production process in
response to various rare earth element (REE) contents were investigated, and their consequential impact on the corrosion
resistance of steel was evaluated. The results indicated that with increasing rare earth content from 0 to 50.37 9 10-6, REE
inclusions were preferentially generated in the following order when they were modiﬁed: REAlO 3 ? RE2O2S ? RE2O3.
The inclusion size and density were negatively correlated and positively correlated with the REE content in 304 stainless
steel, respectively. The formation of Mg–Al spinel and MnS in the continuously cast billet was suppressed by the addition
of rare earth elements. The voltage potential differences between the REE-modiﬁed inclusions and the steel substrate were
obviously less than those between the unmodiﬁed inclusions and the steel substrate. The addition of rare earth elements
contributed to the improvement in the corrosion resistance of 304 stainless steel.

1 Introduction
Stainless steel is commonly employed in a variety of
maritime applications, including ships, marine oil and gas
extraction and transportation systems, offshore platforms,
and other related ﬁelds. Compared with the terrestrial
environment, ocean currents change drastically with the
season. Stainless steel is subject to the low-frequency
reciprocating stress and impact force generated by waves,
tides and currents, and its service environment is extremely
harsh. The key considerations of its physical properties
include toughness, strength, plasticity, welding perfor-
mance, corrosion resistance, and uniformity [ 1–8].
Rare earth elements (REEs) are highly important in the
metallurgical industry and are considered special additions
to improve the properties and quality of steels. Rare earth
elements can exist in steel in three primary forms. The ﬁrst
form is the uniform dissolution of rare earth elements in the
steel solution in the solid state, similar to other conven-
tional alloying elements. The second form involves rare
earth elements acting as reactive elements, which are
combined with the sulfur and oxygen in steel to generate
nonmetallic compounds. The third form involves the for-
mation of compounds with manganese and vanadium in
steel [ 9, 10]. These compounds precipitate as nonuniform
nucleation cores before the steel solidiﬁes. For steel,
nonuniform nucleation reduces the degree of subcooling
during crystallization, reﬁnes the solidiﬁcation structure,

fatigue toughness of steel by optimizing the microstructure
and inclusions [ 11–14].
In recent years, many scholars have conducted extensive
studies on the effects of rare earth treatments on steel.
Meng et al. [ 15] produced high manganese steel containing
rare-earth yttrium using a vacuum induction furnace, and
the composite inclusions in the high manganese steel were
analysed in a multidimensional structure. After modiﬁca-
tion with rare-earth yttrium, the MnS–Al 2O3 composite
inclusions in the steel were transformed into Y 2S3–Y2O2S
composite inclusions with smaller size and near-spherical
shape. In laboratory experiments conducted by Zhang et al.
[16], researchers examined the transient changes in inclu-
sions in Si–Mn stainless steel with the addition of
40 9 10-6, 100 9 10-6, and 400 9 10-6 cerium. As the
cerium content increased, the inclusions changed from
having a composition of Al 2O3–SiO2–MnO–CaO to
Ce2O3–Al2O3–SiO2–MnO–CaO to Ce 2O3–CeS. Luo et al.
[17] proposed that the evolution of Al 2O3 inclusions
formed by Al deoxidation in 20CrNiMo gear steel followed
the route of Al 2O3 ? CeAlO3 ? Ce2O2S ? Ce2O2-
S ? CeS. Zhang et al. [ 18] examined the impacts of Ce
reﬁning agents on the solidiﬁcation structure and
mechanical properties of ferritic stainless steel and dis-
cussed the associated mechanism. The results showed that
the addition of 0.011 wt.% Ce and 0.023 wt.% Ce reﬁning
agents substantially enhanced the ductility and toughness
of the material. Kim et al. [ 19] studied the impacts of rare
earth additions on the pitting resistance characteristics of
super duplex stainless steels. The addition of rare earth
elements to the base alloy led to the formation of (Mn, Cr,
Si, Al, Ce) oxides and (Mn, Cr, Si, Ce) oxides, enhancing
the resistance to pitting and diminishing the favored
interfacial region for pitting initiation. Wang et al. [ 20]
characterized the pitting corrosion behavior of 13Cr4Ni
martensitic stainless steel (BASE) and rare-earth modiﬁed
stainless steel (REM) in a 0.1 mol/L NaCl solution. The
results showed that REM steel was more susceptible to
inducing metastable pitting nucleation and repassivation
than BASE steel was, and REM steel inhibited the transi-
tion from metastable pitting to stable pitting.
Considering the persistent challenge of fully eliminating
inclusions from steel, it has become imperative to explore
methodologies aiming at mitigating the detrimental effects
of inclusions. In this study, the focus was modifying
inclusions by incorporating rare earth elements into 304
stainless steel, which is widely used in marine industries.
This strategy effectively combated corrosion induced by
inclusions, particularly pitting corrosion, while concur-
rently increasing the resistance of steel to intergranular
corrosion [ 21, 22]. In addition, the extent of inclusion
modiﬁcation achieved after adding various contents of rare
earth elements in practical applications was also explored.
Furthermore, the mechanisms by which the compositions
of inclusions evolved throughout the production processes
of 304 stainless steel in response to various REE contents
were investigated, and their consequential impact on the
corrosion resistance of steel was also evaluated.
2 Experimental methods
Four heats of 304 stainless steel containing low-phosphorus
iron and composite microalloying elements were subjected
to full-scale industrial production processing. This pro-
cesses included smelting iron in a high frequency induction
furnace to produce about 90 t of crude molten steel,
reﬁning the crude molten steel in an argon-oxygen decar-
burization (AOD) converter, treating the reﬁned steel in a
ladle furnace (LF), and then processing the steel through
continuous casting, slab heating, rolling, solid solution
treatment and acid pickling (Fig. 1).
During the LF reﬁning process, pure La and Ce rare
earth metals were packed together in a 304 stainless steel
container and then added into the molten steel. Counter-
weights hung below the container to facilitate the sub-
mergence of the rare earth elements into the molten steel.
The amounts of rare earth elements added to each heat
were as follows: no rare earth elements were added to heat
1; 6 kg of rare earth elements were added to heat 2; 12 kg
of rare earth elements were added to heat 3; and 18 kg of
rare earth elements were added to heat 4. When rare earth
elements were added during alloying, the ratio of Ce to La
was 1:1. Calcium wire was also introduced into the steel at
the appropriate temperature during LF reﬁning, ensuring
that the alloy composition and sulfur content met the
speciﬁed criteria. The treatment involved feeding 1 m of
Ca28Si60 core wire (with a core diameter of /13mm, a
content of 28 wt.% calcium, and a core powder quality of
210 g/m) per tonne of steel at a feeding rate of at least
60 m/min. The detailed chemical composition of the sili-
con–calcium wire is shown in Table 1. Rare earth elements
were added after temperature modiﬁcation and composi-
tion ﬁne-tuning. There was a minimum soft blowing time
of 3 min after addition.
Slag and steel samples were taken from each heat upon
entry into the LF and before exiting the LF. A NexION
1000Gx Inductively Coupled Atomic Emission Spectrom-
eter was employed to ascertain the concentrations of ele-
ments including Al, Si, Ca, Ce, and La in the steel, and the
oxide components in the slag were determined by X-ray
ﬂuorescence spectrometer. A Leco TC500 N/O analyser
was utilised to detect the oxygen content in the steel. An

0.01–1 9 105 Hz using an AC excitation signal amplitude
of 0.005 V. Scanning Kelvin probe force microscopy
(SKPFM) was used to observe the morphologies of typical
inclusions and measure the potential difference between
them and the steel matrix.
In the early stage of the corrosion process, the immer-
sion test is a simple and reliable tool for analysing localised
corrosion mechanisms. The pitting mechanism of
inclusions of each heat of continuous casting billets at
room temperature was studied via immersion tests for 50 h
in 10 vol.% FeCl 3 solution. All tests were conducted at
least three times to ensure reproducible results. The
methods for the immersion treatment experiments,
including solution preparation, frequency of solution
renewal and sample handling procedures, were as follows:
(1) Solution preparation method: 0.37 g of ferric chloride
hexahydrate was dissolved into deionized water, and the
solution volume was adjusted to 100 mL; (2) The fre-
quency of solution renewal: after 10 h of immersion
treatment, the original solution was replaced with new
solution; (3) Sample handling procedure: after the immer-
sion process, the samples were subsequently ground with
400, 800, 1200, 1500, and 2000 grits of sandpapers, and
then polished with a 1 lm diamond paste until there were
no scratches. At the end of etching and polishing, the
sample surface was rinsed clean using alcohol solution.

3.1.2 Continuous casting process
During the cooling process of the continuously cast billets,
the cooling rate of the outer layer is greater than that of the
inner layer. The cooling rate of the continuously cast billet
exerts inﬂuence on the morphologies and composition
distributions of inclusions. Consequently, the steel samples
were machined from the surface and the centre of the
continuous casting billet for analyzing the characteristics of
inclusions in them.

5 Conclusions
1. In the LF reﬁning process, with increasing rare earth
content from 0 to 50.37 9 10-6, REE inclusions were
preferentially generated in the following order when
they were modiﬁed: REAlO 3 ? RE2O2S ? RE2O3.
With increasing REE content, the amount of the
REAlO3 phase gradually decreased, whereas the con-
tents of the RE 2O2S and RE 2O3 phases in the inclu-
sions increased.
2. The inclusion sizes in the continuously cast billets
obviously decreased after rare earth elements were
added, whereas the inclusion densities increased with
increasing REE contents. In addition, the formation of
Mg–Al spinel and MnS in the continuously cast billet
was suppressed by the addition of rare earth elements.
3. Increasing the rare earth content improved the corro-
sion resistance of 304 stainless steel. The voltage
potential differences between the REE-modiﬁed inclu-
sions and the steel substrate were obviously less than
those between the unmodiﬁed inclusions and the steel
substrate, which decreased the probability of pitting
corrosion initiated by inclusions and minimized the
risk of galvanic coupling corrosion.
4. The incorporation of rare earth elements alters the
mechanism of inclusions-induced pitting. The third
and fourth heat inclusions exhibited a higher
concentration of rare earth elements, thereby markedly
reducing pitting induced by the inclusions.
