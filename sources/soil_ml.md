Title: Machine learning-based corrosion rate prediction of steel embedded in soil

Markdown Content:
## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/)Abstract

Predicting the corrosion rate for soil-buried steel is significant for assessing the service-life performance of structures in soil environments. However, due to the large amount of variables involved, existing corrosion prediction models have limited accuracy for complex soil environment. The present study employs three machine learning (ML) algorithms, i.e., random forest, support vector regression, and multilayer perception, to predict the corrosion current density of soil-buried steel. Steel specimens were embedded in soil samples collected from different regions of the Wisconsin state. Variables including exposure time, moisture content, pH, electrical resistivity, chloride, sulfate content, and mean total organic carbon were measured through laboratory tests and were used as input variables for the model. The current density of steel was measured through polarization technique, and was employed as the output of the model. Of the various ML algorithms, the random forest (RF) model demonstrates the highest predictability (with an RMSE value of 0.01095 A/m 2 and an _R_ 2 value of 0.987). In light of the feature selection method, the electrical resistivity is identified as the most significant feature. The combination of three features (resistivity, exposure time, and mean total organic carbon) is the optimal scenario for predicting the corrosion current density of soil-buried steel.

**Keywords:**Steel corrosion, Corrosion rate, Soil, Machine learning, Random forest

**Subject terms:**Civil engineering, Metals and alloys

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/)Introduction

Carbon steel is widely used in structures in soil environments such as pipelines and bridges (e.g., H-piles). As carbon steel is susceptible to corrosion, corrosion of soil-buried steel has been identified as one of the most significant causes of the failure of pipelines and foundations in aggressive environments[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR1)–[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR5). Corrosion deteriorates the physical and mechanical properties of steel including its surface morphology, cross-sectional area, and strength[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR6)–[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR8). The reduction in the cross-sectional area and the tensile strength of steel directly degrades the load-bearing capacity of steel members and structures[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR7). In addition, the variation in the surface roughness of corroded soil-buried steel significantly influences the soil-structure interfacial shearing property, which in turn affects the bearing capacity of steel foundation in soil environments[9](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR9),[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR10). In this regard, predicting steel corrosion in soil environments is essential in forecasting the deterioration and the remaining service life of structures in soil environments.

The corrosion of steel embedded in soil and other granular media is influenced by various material and environmental factors, including moisture and chloride content in soil[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR3),[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR5),[11](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR11), temperature and pH[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR1),[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR2), and microorganisms such as bacteria[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR2),[12](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR12). Qualitative prediction methods were developed, evaluating the corrosion condition mainly through pH and soil resistivity[13](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR13),[14](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR14). The drawback of qualitative method is its disability to provide accurate corrosion rate. Statistical approaches to develop predictive models for soil-buried steel corrosion are extensively used[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR15)–[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR17). However, the parameters used in empirical models are limited. Typically three to five variables were considered in establishing predictive models[16](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR16)–[18](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR18). In this regard, the application of existing predictive models for soil-buried steel corrosion may be confined to specific scenarios and exposure conditions. However, the corrosion of steel in dissimilar environmental conditions or soil characteristics, or cases with combined different environmental and chemical conditions, can hardly be accurately predicted using such models. Meanwhile, the traditional predictive models cannot be updated by adding experimental data to improve accuracy.

Over the last ten years, there has been a growing popularity of machine learning (ML) models, and they have been applied in multiple disciplines, including biology[19](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR19), metallurgy[20](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR20), the performance of engineering structures[21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR21)–[24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR24), and corrosion[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR25)–[28](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR28). ML is suitable for employment in large amounts of data, where investigation of the relationship between numerous input and output variables is involved. Additionally, ML is used when seemingly different types of data are gathered to establish correlations and predict phenomena. ML can be classified into supervised learning and unsupervised learning[29](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR29),[30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR30). The training process in supervised learning requires labeling each input with its desired output value, also known as features and targets. Supervised learning algorithms such as random forest (RF), support vector regressor (SVR), and multilayer perceptron (MLP) are commonly employed.

On the other hand, unsupervised learning functions through classifying the input into classes or clusters without labeling. Supervised learning, as a popular ML technique, has been preliminarily explored in pipeline corrosion[31](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR31),[32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR32), atmospheric corrosion[33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR33),[34](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR34), and corrosion of steel embedded in mortars and concrete[26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR26). The emerging application of ML allows more effective predictions of corrosion activity affected by complex parameters. Supervised learning algorithms including RF, SVR and MLP demonstrate superior performance in corrosion prediction, becoming the highly-used methods in this field over the years[35](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR35). These algorithms yield fair accuracy in the corrosion prediction of diverse environments such as steel corrosion in seawater[36](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR36), steel corrosion in cementitious materials[26](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR26), and the corrosion of other alloys[37](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR37). Nevertheless, research using ML techniques to quantitively predict the corrosion rate of steel in soil environments is limited[38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR38). The accuracy of ML algorithms and the significance of different variables are still unknown in predicting the corrosion of soil-buried steels.

This study aimed to develop an ML-based model for predicting soil-buried steel corrosion. In light of the capability in addressing vast amount of data and the accuracy in corrosion-related researches over the years, the random forest (RF), support vector regressor (SVR), and multilayer perceptron (MLP) methods were employed in the present study. Three ML models were investigated and their performance in soil-buried steel corrosion prediction were compared. Exposure time, moisture content, pH, resistivity, chloride, sulfate and sulfide contents, and mean total organic carbon (MTOC) were utilized as the inputs for the models. The corrosion current density of soil-buried steel was the output of the models. The optimization of the ML-based model was performed based on feature selection and the Pearson correlation coefficient.

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/)Materials and methods

Steel H-Piles are commonly used in bridge structures to resist vertical and lateral loads. These piles are manufactured from a variety of materials, including carbon steel. Although carbon steel is susceptible to corrosion, it is widely chosen for its low cost and high strength. Nonetheless, concerns often arise regarding the corrosion of steel piles[38](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR38),[39](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR39). On September 25, 2013, Pier 22 of the Leo Frigo Bridge experienced a vertical displacement of approximately 2 feet. This vertical movement resulted in a reduction in structural capacity due to the loss of the section of the steel H-piles supporting the pier[40](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR40). To gain insight into the corrosion activity of soil-buried steel and to establish a framework for predicting the service life of such structures, soil samples were collected from the state of Wisconsin. To ensure a wide variety of sample properties, soils in three different locations across Wisconsin were collected, i.e., Wausau in central Wisconsin, Madison in south-central of Wisconsin, and Milwaukee in southeastern Wisconsin and also a port of entry on Lake Michigan, as demonstrated in Fig.[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig1). Laboratory cells were prepared using the collected soil samples and steel specimens with chemical compositions similar to those used in the production of steel piles (according to ASTM A572-50[41](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR41)).Figure 1Locations for the soil sample collection.

### Electrochemical tests and data collection

For electrochemical testing, a three-electrode measurement setup was utilized. This setup comprised a specimen functioning as the working electrode, a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) serving as the reference electrode, and a 316 stainless steel sheet acting as the counter electrode. The corrosion potential of all specimens was measured against the SCE. The linear polarization resistance (LPR) method was utilized to calculate the corrosion current densities of the steel specimens. All measurements were started 24 h after the steel specimens were embedded in the soils and were conducted at two-week intervals.

To determine corrosion current from the results of the LPR test, the values of _β_ a and _β_ c are needed to calculate the Stern-Geary Constant, _B_,[47](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR47), which was required for calculating the corrosion current densities based on the polarization resistance (_R_ p). Thus, the Tafel extrapolation was carried out on the specimens two days after their placement in the soils. The scan was started at − 500 mV below the corrosion potential and was increased to + 500 mV above the corrosion potential, with a scan rate of 0.166 mV/s. All the electrochemical measurements were performed in laboratory under room temperature (~ 22°C).

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/)Machine learning models

### Machine learning approaches

In ML, algorithms are procedures or computational processes that address input data and produce an output. Algorithms allow computers to learn patterns or structures from the datasets, thereby making predictions or performing tasks. The algorithms used in this work are explained herein.

RF is a machine learning algorithm that is used for classification and regression tasks. It's an ensemble algorithm, meaning it builds multiple decision trees (DT) and merges them together to get a more accurate and stable prediction. As such, it composes of a new training sample set to train DT. DT refers to an algorithm with a structure similar to a tree graph, as depicted in Fig.[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig2). It splits data into subsets based on feature values, creating a tree-like structure. Each internal node represents a feature, each branch represents a decision rule, and each leaf node represents an outcome. It branches existing samples according to given features and eventually builds a tree, where the root node of the tree is the feature with the minimum Gini coefficient selected according to Eq.([1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Equ1)) [48](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR48):1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$\text{Gini }(D)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{c}{P}_{i}^{2}$\end{document}

$$
\text{Gini} \left(\right. D \left.\right) = 1 - \sum_{i = 1}^{c} P_{i}^{2}
$$

where Gini (_D_) represents the Gini coefficient of dataset _D_, _c_ represents the number of categories in dataset _D_, and _P_ _i_ represents the proportion of the _i_ th category sample in dataset _D_.Figure 2Schematic diagram of decision tree (DT).

The nodes of DT (i.e., features) are then branched down in the order of Gini coefficients from small to large, and the tree leaves (i.e., the end of the tree) represent the final decision. For instance, as shown in Fig.[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig2), the nodes of DT are branched down according to the input features, i.e., pH, moisture content, resistivity, chloride, etc., in the present study. If pH < 7, then the prediction results (i.e., corrosion rate) is Target 1; If pH > 7 and moisture content < 10%, then Target 2; and so on. DT can be applied to datasets with numerous cases and variables, showing a high degree of interpretability[49](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR49). The new training sample set used to train decision trees in RF is created through repeatedly and randomly selecting samples from the original training sample set by bootstrap. Multiple decision trees are then generated by repeating the above steps. RF is essentially an upgrade of the DT algorithm. It combines multiple decision trees, where the establishment of each tree relies on independently extracted samples. Therefore, RF is also an integrated learning model[50](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR50).

Feature selection is implicitly performed during the construction of DT within the RF. The contribution of each input feature to the RF model can be evaluated by calculating the importance of each feature. In addition, the correlation between two variables can be assessed by the Pearson correlation coefficient (_r_), which is the ratio of covariance and standard deviation between the variables. A large value of _r_ means that the change of one variable can be well-known from the other[51](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR51),[52](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR52). The calculation of _r_ between two variables is expressed as Eq.([2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Equ2)). The coefficient ranges from − 1 to 1. $$
r = \frac{\sum \left(\right. x_{i} - \bar{x} \left.\right) \left(\right. y_{i} - \bar{y} \left.\right)}{\sqrt{\sum \left(x_{i},-,\bar{x}\right)^{2}} \sqrt{\sum \left(y_{i},-,\bar{y}\right)^{2}}}
$$

where _x_ _i_ and _y_ _i_ are the values of the _i_ th and _j_ th variables, respectively; _‾x_ and _‾y_ represent the corresponding averages.

SVR is a powerful tool for predicting continuous values by finding an optimal function that approximates the relationship between input features and target values within a specified margin of error. It leverages support vectors to create a robust and generalizable model, making it suitable for various regression tasks. Figure[3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig3) schematically illustrates the structure of SVR algorithm. Three layers are included in the framework, i.e., the input layer, the kernel functions, and the output layer. The basic principle of SVR is to mathematically map the input sample _X_ to high-dimensional feature space _F_ through nonlinear function _φ_(_x_) (i.e., kernel functions) to better fit data[53](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR53). The function of SVR is established according to the principle of minimizing structural risk, as shown in Eq.([3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Equ3)) [54](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR54):3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$y^{\prime} = w \cdot \varphi \left( x \right) + b$\end{document}

$$
y^{'} = w \cdot \varphi \left(x\right) + b
$$

where _w_ refers to the weight vector, _w_ ∈ F, _b_ is the bias vector, _b_ ∈ R (real numbers), and _y_′ is the predicted value. The SVR algorithm allows a divergence between the output and the actual value, creating a "spacer band" on both sides of the linear function. The loss is calculated when samples fall outside the spacer band. The model is optimized by minimizing the width and total loss of the spacer band.Figure 3Schematic diagram of SVR.

MLP stems from the principle of neural networks in biology. The MLP algorithm simulates the processing mode of the nervous system of the human brain to complex information through fully understanding the structure of the human brain and the external stimulus–response mechanism. An MLP is a type of artificial neural network used in machine learning for both classification and regression tasks. It's called a "multi-layer" perceptron because it has multiple layers of nodes (neurons), which allow it to learn and model complex relationships in data. As shown in Fig.[4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig4), the structure of MLP includes an input layer, a hidden layer, and an output layer. Each input variable (_x_ _i_) is connected to a node in the next layer (i.e., the first hidden layer), and each connection is assigned a weight (_ω_ _ij_). The obtained result (_F_ _i_) is input into the first hidden layer by weighing and summing _x_ _i_. Each layer transforms the input in some way, allowing the network to learn intricate patterns. For example, the first hidden layer may learn basic features like moisture content, pH, resistivity, etc. and the relationships from the input features. The second hidden layer may combine these features into more complex ones, like the relationships from the combined moisture content and resistivity features. The third hidden layer further refines and combines the features from the second hidden layer. Finally, the summation of results after weighing is transformed through an activation function (e.g., the sigmoid function) to obtain the output of the first hidden layer (_h_ _i_), as expressed in Eq.([4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Equ4)) [55](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR55):4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}${h}_{i}=\frac{1}{1+{\text{e}}^{-{F}_{i}}}$\end{document}

$$
h_{i} = \frac{1}{1 + \text{e}^{- F_{i}}}
$$

where _F_ _i_ is the weighted sum result of _x_ _i_, and _h_ _i_ is the output of the first hidden layer. This process demonstrates that the MLP can learn and model the complex relationships between environmental factors and the corrosion rate of steel, improving its predictions with each layer of abstraction and interaction. As such, MLP become a popular ML method in corrosion prediction[56](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR56). Notedly, the training procedure of MLP requires adjusting the weights of interconnected nodes to minimize the mean square error between predicted and actual result values[57](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR57).Figure 4Schematic diagram of MLP architecture.

### Machine learning implementation methodology

The structure of the ML model is constructed by hyperparameters (e.g., the maximum tree depth and the number of decision trees[58](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR58),[59](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR59), the penalty coefficient, and the kernel parameter[21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR21),[60](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR60),[61](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR61)). The hyperparameters directly affect the prediction accuracy of each algorithm on datasets[62](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR62). To achieve the most accurate prediction, the grid search method is widely utilized to obtain the optimal hyperparameters due to its simplicity[61](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR61),[63](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR63). Once hyperparameters are established, the model can undergo training with the training set and subsequent evaluation using the test set. The following steps were used to develop the model of the abovementioned machine learning process[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR25),[32](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR32),[64](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR64): (1) data preprocessing, (2) feature engineering, (3) data splitting, (4) model hyperparameter selection, and (5) model training and evaluation, as schematically displayed in Fig.[5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig5). Each step is discussed below.Figure 5Schematic diagram of the ML process.

_Step 1_ Data preprocessing: The data employed in this study presents high integrity and only contains a small number of missing values. In this regard, the missing values were directly deleted. After processing, there are 1428 sets of data left.

_Step 2_ Feature engineering: To prevent the dependence on variables with large dimensionality in the model calculation, standardization was utilized to eliminate this effect and make the prediction more robust.

_Step 3_ Data splitting: One part of the experimental data set was used as a training model, and the other was used to test the model's performance. Data partitioning requires a balance between maximizing the model's performance and avoiding overfitting. In this regard, 80% of data were employed for model training, while 20% were used for model testing.

_Step 4_ Selection of hyperparameters: The grid search technique was used to determine the optimal hyperparameters for each model, aiming for improved prediction outcomes. The hyperparameters under consideration for the RF model include the number of estimators and the maximum depth of the tree. The hyperparameters subject to exploration in the SVR model include the penalty coefficient (C) and the kernel parameter (γ). The penalty coefficient controls the trade-off between maximizing the margin and minimizing the training error. A large value of _C_ indicates less tolerance for training error, possibly leading to overfitting, whereas a small value of _C_ emphasizes maximizing the classification margin. The kernel parameter is linked to the count of support vectors, influencing the pace of training and prediction. The hyperparameters explored by the MLP model encompass the number of hidden layers and the nodes per hidden layer. Notedly, the optimal hyperparameters were determined through fivefold cross-validation, where the data is randomly divided equally into 5-folds. The ML algorithm was trained to employ the data from 4 folds while the performance is tested on the 5 th fold. This process was repeated 5 times. Each time, a dissimilar fold was chosen for testing[30](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR30),[65](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR65).

_Step 5_ Model training and evaluation: The training process of each model was proceeded after tuning the hyperparameters. In terms of evaluation for the performance of models, the root mean squared error (RMSE), the mean absolute error (MAE), the coefficient of determination (_R_ 2), and the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) were employed, as shown in Eqs. ### Feature selection

Including all relevant factors potentially improves the fitting results of the model. Nevertheless, the model's adaptability can be limited by containing excessive features, especially in dealing with a small dataset. In this regard, feature selection is crucial in developing ML models.

Based on the RF algorithm, the contribution of each input feature was obtained, which was denoted as the importance presented in Fig.[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig6). Resistivity occupied the highest contribution, which was 36.8%. It indicates the prominent significance of resistivity in soil-buried steel corrosion[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR1). The corrosiveness of soil has been reported to be the reason related to almost 70% of failure of buried iron-based metal pipelines[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR1),[66](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR66), and resistivity is acknowledged to be a representative indicator of soil corrosivity. Laboratory investigations and field surveys suggest that a small resistivity value generally facilitates the passage of electrical current, thereby increasing the corrosion rate[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR1),[67](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR67), which is in accord with the results of this study shown in Fig.[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig7)a. The soil resistivity directly depends on the porosity, the moisture content, and soluble ions. The increment of moisture content and chloride concentration reduces the resistivity of soil. In this regard, a positive relationship was demonstrated between the corrosion current density and the moisture content and chloride, as shown in Fig.[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig7)d and e, respectively.Figure 6Importance of features to corrosion current density calculated through RF-based model.Figure 7The relationship between corrosion current density and (**a**) resistivity, (**b**) MTOC, (**c**) exposure time, (**d**) moisture content, and (**e**) chloride.

Microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) is a pivotal corrosion process for steel buried in soil. Heterotrophic microorganisms consume organic carbon for their growth, thereby accelerating corrosion through increasing soil acidity[67](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR67). Anaerobic bacteria, e.g., sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB), tend to transform the soil into a highly aggressive environment by forming hydrogen sulfide, thereby enhancing the corrosion process[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR1),[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR2),[68](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR68). As demonstrated in Fig.[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig7)b, the corrosion current density of soil-buried steel presents a positive relationship with the content of MTOC.

Steel corrosion is acknowledged to be a time-variant process[25](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR25),[27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR27). The performance of ML-based corrosion prediction models subjected to various environments (e.g., atmosphere, seawater, concrete environments) is considerably increased regarding time as an input feature[27](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR27),[69](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR69). A nonnegligible contribution of exposure time to corrosion rate was indicated in the present study by the importance of 15.7%, as presented in Fig.[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig6). The value of corrosion current density was calculated through linear polarization resistance. In light of the data collected through experimental investigations, the relationship between corrosion current density and input features was obtained, as shown in Fig.[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig7). As can be seen, although the value of corrosion current density scattered over a broad range at each exposure time, the evolution of its maximum value displayed an initial increase followed by a decrease until it reached a steady state.

According to the importance results (Fig.[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig6)), five features (i.e., resistivity, MTOC, exposure time, moisture content, and chloride) were selected as the candidates for key variables, with an individual contribution of over 10% and a cumulative contribution of 98.9% to corrosion current density.

In addition to the contribution of each feature, the correlations between variables were evaluated through the Pearson correlation coefficient, as demonstrated in Fig.[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig8). The absolute value of the Pearson correlation coefficient was between 0 and 1. Closer proximity to 1 indicates a stronger correlation. A strong correlation between two variables was determined once the absolute value of the Pearson correlation coefficient was greater than 0.7[33](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR33). In this regard, seven groups of strongly correlated features were identified, i.e., resistivity and moisture content, resistivity and chloride, resistivity and MTOC, resistivity and sulfate, MTOC and moisture content, moisture content, and chloride, moisture content and sulfate. In light of this, the candidate key features can be further filtered by removing the variable that occupied a smaller importance in each strongly correlated group. For instance, the absolute value of the Pearson correlation coefficient between resistivity and sulfate content was 0.73, indicating a strong correlation. As demonstrated by the importance in Fig.[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig6), the contribution of sulfate content to the corrosion rate was less than that for the resistivity. In this regard, the sulfate content can be discarded from the key feature selection. Based on the importance and Pearson correlation coefficient results, the key features were identified as resistivity, exposure time, MTOC, moisture content, and chloride concentration.Figure 8Pearson correlation coefficient of each variable. Values marked with the rectangle demonstrate a strong correlation.

### Sensitivity analysis

To understand the relationship between the input and the output parameter, and to ensure the robustness and identify errors in the model[24](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR24), a sensitivity analysis was carried out. According to the importance of features shown in Fig.[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig6), the resistivity, MTOC, moisture content, and chloride yielded an individual contribution of over 10% to corrosion current density. Therefore, the sensitivity analysis was performed on the trained RF-based model to study the effects of resistivity, MTOC, moisture content and chloride on the corrosion current density. During the sensitivity analysis, only one parameter was changed while others kept constant. In addition, four exposure time periods were considered, including 3 days, 30 days, 100 days, 400 days, respectively. As demonstrated in Fig.[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig10), positive relationships existed between the current density and the MTOC, the moisture content, the chloride, respectively, while a negative relationship existed between the current density and the resistivity. This agreed with the experimental results shown in Fig.[7](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig7). Similar relationships were obtained in the cases of different exposure time. Notedly, with same parameter,the absolute value of current density under different exposure time varied. For instance, the current density when resistivity equaled 150 Ω∙m dramatically increased from 3 to 30 days (see Fig.[10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig10)a). It indicated the time-dependent characteristic of soil-buried steel corrosion.Figure 10Effect of (**a**) resistivity, (**b**) MTOC, (**c**) moisture content, and (**d**) chloride of soil on corrosion current densities.

### Optimization of model

Five features, i.e., resistivity, exposure time, MTOC, moisture content, and chloride concentration, were identified as key variables through feature selection. Amongst the key features, resistivity showed a strong correlation with the other three features (i.e., MTOC, moisture content, chloride concentration), as indicated in Fig.[8](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig8). Additionally, the importance of resistivity surpassed that of the MTOC, moisture content, and chloride concentration. Exposure time demonstrated a relatively weak correlation with other variables, though nonnegligible importance, as shown in Fig.[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig6). In this regard, resistivity and exposure time are the most relevant variables among the five key features. The RF-based model's performance in different feature combinations cases was evaluated to obtain the optimal prediction model, as listed in Table [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Tab5). Nevertheless, a relatively strong correlation between resistivity and moisture content was demonstrated by the data collected in the present study (i.e., Pearson correlation coefficient: − 0.98). It is possible to infer that including moisture content in the model introduces redundancy, thereby leading to a slight decrease in the model's performance. Based on the evaluation of models listed in Table [5](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Tab5), the combination of three features (resistivity, exposure time, MTOC) was identified as the optimal scenario for predicting the current corrosion density. This combination yielded smaller values of RMSE and MAE, in addition to a high value of _R_ 2 with fewer features, compared to other cases.

### Application and limitations of ML model in comparisons with existing empirical models

The aim of the present study is to develop ML-based model which can accurately predict the soil-buried steel corrosion. To further evaluate the predictive accuracy of the proposed model, comparison was made with existing empirical models. Table [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Tab6) lists the mathematical expressions of three widely-used empirical models for soil-buried steel corrosion. As can be seen, the RF-based optimized model containing the combination of three features (resistivity, exposure time and MTOC) reaches the highest value of _R_ 2 (0.983), with RMSE and MAE values of 0.00632 and 0.00654. The RF-based model which consists of all seven features in this study also yields a high accuracy with _R_ 2, RMSE and MAE values of 0.918, 0.00316, and 0.00245. With respect to the empirical models, their performance is dramatically poor. The highest value of _R_ 2 in the empirical models is 0.070, far below the accuracy of RF-based model. The empirical models are built by establishing the regression model between the corrosion rate and single or multiple variables[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR17). While the trend between variable and corrosion rate is reasonable and beneficial in approximately evaluating the corrosion, the scatter of the data is noneligible, rendering an _R_ 2 value lower than 0.5[17](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR17). This is due to that the number of variables considered in the empirical models is relatively limited. In addition, the variable involved in existing empirical models may not be important in predicting the corrosion rate of soil-buried steel. For instance, pH has been employed in the prediction model proposed by Ref.[15](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR15), while it demonstrates a quite small contribution to the corrosion current density, as shown in Fig.[6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Fig6). In this regard, although the empirical models are quite convenient in application (i.e., in predicting the corrosion rate of soil-buried steel in engineering structures), its accuracy is hardly secured. As compared in Table [6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#Tab6), the RF-based models, on the contrary, exhibit superior performance in the high value of _R_ 2 and the low values of RMSE and MAE. Notedly, although a large amount of data was collected in the present study, variables like temperature and particle size of soil may affect the corrosion of soil-buried steel[1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR1),[2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR2),[71](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/#CR71), which is outside the scope of the present study. The methodology and result of ML model optimization in this study promisingly guide the development and application of a corrosion prediction model for soil-buried steel. As such, the evolution of load-bearing capacity for soil structures such as foundations can be accurately predicted.

## [](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303726/)Conclusions

The present study developed a machine learning ML-based corrosion prediction model of soil-buried steel. Three popular ML algorithms, including random forest (RF), support vector regression (SVR), and multilayer perception (MLP), were employed. The model used various factors as input variables to predict the corrosion current density of steel specimens in soil, including moisture content, pH, electrical resistivity, chloride, sulfate, and sulfide concentrations, mean total organic carbon (MTOC), and exposure time.

Results showed that the RF-based model exhibited the highest accuracy in predicting the corrosion current density of soil-buried steel compared with the other algorithms. The value of RMSE for the prediction using the RF model was as low as 0.01095 A/m 2, with an _R_ 2 value of 0.987.

In light of the RF algorithm, the electrical resistivity is identified as the most significant feature for predicting the corrosion current density of soil-buried steel. Based on feature selection and Pearson correlation coefficient, the RF-based model was optimized with fewer features, larger values of _R_ 2, and comparable lower values of RMSE and MAE. The combination of three features (resistivity, exposure time, MTOC) is the optimal scenario for predicting the corrosion current density of soil-buried steel.
