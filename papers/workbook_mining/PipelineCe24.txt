ABSTRACT 
 
Minor additions of rare earth elements (REEs) have been shown to improve various steel properties. In 
particular, Cerium (Ce), as a relatively abundant and inexpensive element, has received attention as a 
potentially beneficial minor alloying addition for a variety of steel applications, including pipeline materials. 
The objective of this work is to investigate the effect of Ce on corrosion performance of experimental 
pipeline steel. Electrochemical corrosion experiments were carried out on steel without and with 0.0057, 
0.0164, and 0.0263 wt.% of Ce in 3.5 wt.% NaCl saturated with CO2 at 20 °C. For comparison purposes, 
X100 carbon steel specimens machined from an experimental pipe with a yield strength of 731 MPa were 
tested under the same conditions. The electrochemical techniques employed included: potentiodynamic 
polarization (PDP), linear polarization resistance (LPR), and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy 
(EIS). In addition, post-corrosion surface characterization was performed using scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) equipped with energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The corrosion of base 
metal without Ce was significantly lower (0.15 mm/y) compared to carbon steel X100 (0.9 mm/yr). The 
electrochemical results showed that adding Ce accelerates corrosion rates compared to base metal 
without Ce. No pitting corrosion of tested steel was detected.

INTRODUCTION 
High-pressure steel pipeline is a common, cost-effective method for transporting hydrogen and captured 
carbon dioxide (CO2) from its point of capture to storage sites [1]. In the past decade, high-strength steel 
pipeline technology has been developing in response to rapidly increasing energy demand. In general, 
carbon steels (X60, X65, and X80) are the main commercial materials used in constructing CO2 
transportation pipelines [2]. Higher steel grades are being considered for future use in higher pressure 
transmission pipelines. However, high-strength steels can experience sudden failure due to hydrogen 
accumulation in the lattice or in defects, such as dislocations, grain boundaries, and carbides. In addition, 
steel pipelines are susceptible to corrosion during the transportation of CO2, due to the presence of 
moisture and corrosive impurities such as H2S and O2.The unique metallurgical microstructure at pipeline 
welds, which is combined with a synergism of hydrogen and stress, makes the area prone to corrosion, 
such as hydrogen-induced degradation, resulting in pipeline failure. Therefore, it is important to 
manufacture a more robust pipeline material for transporting hydrogen and captured CO2. In addition, in 
the pipeline transportation system, the steel pipeline microstructure plays an important role in the 
corrosion process. Therefore, another method to mitigate CO2 corrosion is by using corrosion-resistant 
steel pipelines. 
To preserve mechanical properties and modify or remove the formation of MnS inclusions in steel, rare 
earth elements (REEs) can be added to the steel during the manufacturing process [3, 4]. Several studies 
proved that REEs in the steel can purify the molten steel [3], modify inclusions [5, 6, 7], and improve the 
structure and properties of steels [6, 8]. It was found that the type and size distribution of inclusions in 
the steel changes with the difference in added Ce content [5, 6, 7]. Wan et al. [5] observed the formation 
of CeAlO3-Ce2O2S inclusions when the added Ce content in the steel was below 60 ppm. However, the 
inclusions were mainly CexSy-Ce2O2S when the added Ce content in the steel was 95 ppm. In the past 
10 years, Ce has been one of the most investigated rare-earth salts and has been successfully used to 
control aqueous corrosion of steel [9]. In addition, REEs have been also found to have a positive effect 
on the corrosion resistance of steel exposed to marine environments [4]. Montemor et al. [9] evaluated 
the composition and corrosion behavior of galvanized steel treated with Ce in 0.3 wt.% NaCl. The author 
observed formation of Ce oxide and/or hydroxide on galvanized steel which constrained the cathodic 
reduction of oxygen and the anodic dissolution of zinc, leading to a decrease of the corrosion rate [9]. 
Many studies have been conducted to investigate the effects of REE-modified inclusions on the initiation 
of pitting corrosion in steels [10, 11, 7]. Lijie et al. [10] showed that REE content of 0.0065−0.016 wt% in 
weathering steel modified inclusion which significantly improved the resistance to pitting corrosion. Zhang 
et al. [12] observed dissolution of the steel matrix after adding Ce element to stainless steel which caused 
pitting corrosion to occur.  
Very limited studies on the effect of Ce addition on corrosion behavior of steel in CO2 aqueous 
environments have been performed. Therefore, in this work, efforts have been made to investigate the 
effect of minor Ce additions on corrosion behavior of experimental pipeline steel immersed in an aqueous 
CO2 electrolyte. The susceptibility of experimental pipeline steels to pitting corrosion was investigated 
with potentiodynamic polarization and immersion corrosion tests combined with SEM/EDS analysis.

Electrochemical measurement 
In this study, conventional electrochemical measurements were performed on five experimental pipeline 
steels (H20B, H21, H22, H23, and X100), in 3.5 wt.% NaCl saturated with CO2 at 20 °C. The corrosion 
behavior of the pipeline steels was studied using a flat electrochemical cell with three electrodes 
configuration (Figure 2). Steel specimens with exposed surface area of 1 cm2 were used as the working 
electrode. A saturated calomel reference electrode (SCE) was connected externally to the cell via a 
Luggin capillary with a porous glass tip. The counter electrode was made of a platinum mesh. The solution 
was deoxygenated by purging with CO2 gas for 2 to 3 hours. After the dissolved oxygen was eliminated, 
the pH of the test solution reached an equilibrium pH of 3.8 to 3.9. All the corrosion tests were conducted 
at ambient temperature (20 °C ±0.1). Before the corrosion test, each specimen was ground using silicon

Surface Analysis 
After 24 hours of exposure to CO2 environment, the samples were taken out, rinsed with isopropanol, 
dried, and stored in a desiccator for surface analyses. The exposed surface was characterized by 
scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) using a using a 
JEOL JSM-IT700HR SEM.

Linear Polarization Resistance Corrosion Rate 
The LPR and OCP measurements were carried out to study the effect of Ce on the corrosion behavior of 
tested steel after 24 hours of immersion in 3.5% NaCl solution. The results are shown in Figure 4.  The 
corrosion rate for the steel without Ce (H20B) is obviously lower than that of the steel with Ce. The 
corrosion rates of steel increased with the increase of Ce content in steel when Ce content was in the 
range of 0.0%–0.0164% (0–164 ppm). Insignificant changes in corrosion rates were observed with Ce 
content in the range of 0.0164%–0.0263% (164–263 ppm). Steel X100 revealed a higher corrosion rate 
(~1 mmy) compared to the steels with and without Ce. Therefore, the corrosion resistance of the steel 
without Ce (H20B, 0.16 mm/y) is obviously better than that of the steel with Ce and X100 (~1 mm/y) 
(Figure 4a).  
As shown in Figure 4, it seems that there is a threshold Ce concentration (0.0164%) whereby Ce will 
accelerate corrosion rate. Under the CO2 aqueous environment, the main cathodic reaction is a reduction 
of H+ and evolution of H2 (Equation 4) and the main anodic process is the dissolution of iron to Fe2+ 
(Equation 3). With time, if the saturation level of the solution is above 1, iron and carbonate ions will react, 
and iron carbonate will precipitate (Equation 8), which slows down dissolution of iron and provides 
corrosion protection. As it was explained in the previous section, Ce can increase the rate of desorption 
of H atoms, ultimately increasing the reduction rate of hydrogen and decreasing hydrogen permeation 
through the steel [19]; therefore, accelerating the corrosion rate of the steel. In addition, it was shown in 
literature that manganese sulfides were replaced by Ce inclusions (CexSy-Ce2O2S) in the steel, which 
may affect its corrosion behavior [5]. The OCP (Figure 5b) followed a similar trend with and without Ce. 
However, the OCP shifted slightly to more cathodic potential in the presence of Ce.

Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy 
EIS was carried out to complement the LPR results and investigate the corrosion behavior of steel 
substrates under exposure to 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution saturated with CO2. Figure 5 depicts the impedance 
spectra obtained on steel with and without Ce after 24 hours of exposure to 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution 
saturated with CO2. All impedance spectra showed a depressed capacitive loop (Figure 5a), indicating 
double-layer capacitance and a one-time constant (Figure 5b) that can be assigned to the charge transfer 
reactions occurring on the steels. Depressed semi-circles are characteristic for heterogeneous surface 
roughness and the non-uniform distribution of current density on the surface [20, 21]. The low-frequency 
limit of the impedance decreased with increasing the Ce content, indicating decrease in polarization 
resistance, and increase in corrosion rate. The charge transfer resistance of steel H20B is higher (by 
factor 2) than the value for steel H22. Following the Ce content order, the corrosion resistance is higher 
for steel without Ce (HB20) and lower for the steel with 0.0263% Ce (H23). This result also showed that 
steel without Ce has a better corrosion resistance compared to X100 and steels with Ce. The impedance 
results confirm the conclusion obtained from potentiodynamic curves and LPR.

Surface Characterization 
Figure 6 shows SEM images of typical inclusions for the four experimental pipeline steels. As described 
in a concurrent study [] the inclusions are predominantly Mn-rich sulfides and Mn-Si-Al-rich oxides in the 
absence of Ce. The added Ce preferentially reacts with oxygen and sulfur impurities, gradually modifying 
the oxide and sulfide inclusions to contain higher levels of Ce (observed as bright contrast regions in 
Figure 6). The 0.0263% (263 ppm) Ce addition results in a nearly stoichiometric amount of Ce, given the 
oxygen and sulfur impurities level of the steel. As a result, virtually all the inclusions present in the 263 
ppm Ce steel consisted of Ce oxysulfide, Ce2O2S. Figure 7 shows surface SEM and corresponding EDS 
maps of each of the steels after the corrosion test. The corrosion product was observed for all samples, 
evidenced by the oxygen maps. Carbon was also detected by EDS mapping, however in all cases 
quantitative EDS indicated O:C ratio much lower than 3:1, ruling out the possibility of iron carbonate 
(FeCO3) and implying the corrosion products consisted of iron oxide or oxyhydroxide. XRD 
measurements of the corroded surface (data not shown) did not reveal any crystalline products; hence 
the precise identity of the corrosion product is unclear. For the Ce-free and 0.0057% (57 ppm) Ce 
samples, parts of the surface were covered by a relatively thick corrosion product (seen as O-rich regions 
in the EDS maps), while large regions with little or no corrosion product were also observed. Meanwhile, 
the 0.0164% (164 ppm) Ce sample contained some regions with relatively thick corrosion product and 
other regions with relatively thinner corrosion product (evidenced by less O enrichment), however 
corrosion-free regions such as those observed for the Ce-free and 57 ppm Ce samples were much less 
common. Finally, the 0.0263% (263 ppm) Ce sample and X100 were both uniformly covered by the 
relatively thick corrosion product, as evidenced by O enrichment uniformly across the surface. The extent 
of the corrosion product formation revealed in Figure 7 is in general agreement with the corrosion rates 
calculated from the electrochemical results, confirming the increase in Ce content was associated with a 
higher corrosion rate.

The corroded samples were also characterized by cross-sectional SEM. For all samples, the corrosion 
product was rarely observed on the surface, implying the corrosion product was not strongly bound to 
the steel substrate and was largely lost during preparation of the cross-sections. Nevertheless, some 
regions were found which retained the corrosion product on all samples except X100. For all samples, 
when a corrosion product layer could be found, it always exhibited a thickness of approximately 2 µm, an 
example of this is shown for the 263 Ce sample in Figure 8. The thickness of this corrosion product is in 
good agreement with the extent of corrosion expected based on the electrochemical measurements. That 
is, a corrosion rate of approximately 0.5 mm/y (Figure 4) corresponds to approximately 1−2 µm after the 
24-hour corrosion test. The EDS maps in Figure 8 reveal little or no carbon in the corrosion product layer, 
again confirming the corrosion product consists of iron oxide and/or oxyhydroxide.

CONCLUSIONS 
Investigations of the effect of minor Ce additions on corrosion behavior of experimental pipeline steels 
immersed in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution saturated with CO2 at 20 °C and 1 bar total pressure were performed 
through several electrochemical methods including potentiodynamic polarization, LPR, and EIS, followed 
by SEM/EDS characterization of the corrosion products. The main conclusions from this research are: 
• 
The increase in Ce content was associated with a higher corrosion rate.  
• 
The corrosion resistance of the steel without Ce (H20B, 0.16 mm/y) is obviously better than that 
of the steel with Ce and X100 (~1 mm/y). 
• 
The added Ce preferentially reacted with oxygen and sulfur impurities, gradually modifying the 
oxide and sulfide inclusions to contain higher levels of Ce. 
• 
The surface characterization revealed the formation of the iron oxide and/or oxyhydroxide layers 
on the corroded surfaces.  
• 
Under the conditions tested, no localized corrosion was observed on the surface of the tested 
steel.
