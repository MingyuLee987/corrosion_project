This study deciphers the chloride-induced early-stage corrosion of Cr/RE-microalloyed HRB400
rebars in saturated Ca(OH)₂ pore solutions (0.5–3.5 wt% Cl⁻). Rare-earth addition transforms original
MnS and Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO inclusions into RE–Al–O–S particles, many encapsulated by thin MnS
shells. Preferential MnS dissolution creates occluded cells whose acidiﬁcation simultaneously attacks
the exposed RE–Al–O core and adjacent steel, yet the RE phase markedly retards this sequence.
Across all chloride levels, HRB400-Cr-RE exhibits the highest pitting potential, lowest passive current
density, largest charge-transfer resistance and thinnest passive-ﬁlm donor density; after 7 d
immersion its corrosion rate is one-third that of HRB400 and confocal microscopy conﬁrms the
shallowest pits with the lowest aspect ratios. Elevated Cl⁻ progressively lowers pitting potentials,
raises passive currents, shrinks Nyquist arcs and increases donor densities in all steels, evidencing
accelerated dissolution of inclusions and surrounding matrix under high-chloride conditions.
Reinforced concrete is vital for infrastructure projects like bridges, docks,
tunnels, and subways, owing to its excellent cost-performance ratio and
mechanical strength. It offers exceptional resistance to various environ-
mental stresses, including abrasion, chemical attacks, and fatigue. However,
its long-term durability, critical for serviceability, is compromised by
combined deterioration mechanisms such as chloride-induced corrosion,
carbonation, alkali-silica reactions, and freeze-thaw cycles1–3. Among these,
steel reinforcement corrosion in highly aggressive environments—parti-
cularly marine settings with high salinity, humidity, and wet-dry cycles—
remains a persistent challenge despite extensive research and mitigation
efforts4–6.
In marine environments, chloride ingress represents the predominant
degradation mechanism, accounting for over 60% of reinforced concrete
failures7. Chloride ions—either intrinsic to construction materials or
externally derived from seawater—diffuse through the concrete matrix via
capillary action and permeation, ultimately accumulating at the steel-
concrete interface8,9. While numerous conventional approaches have been
investigated to mitigate chloride attack—including enhancing concrete
density10, increasing cover thickness, applying protective coatings11,
employing electrochemical techniques12, and modifying reinforcement
surfacecharacteristics13,14—these strategies fundamentally modify the steel’s
external environment rather than its intrinsic corrosion resistance. This
critical limitation inherently constrains their long-term protective effec-
tiveness and durability, particularly in harsh marine conditions. A more
fundamental solution lies in alloy design and microstructural optimization
of the steel itself 15–17. By enhancing the material’s inherent corrosion
resistance through compositional and microstructural engineering, this
approach offers a more sustainable and potentially more effective strategy
for combating chloride-induced corrosion in marine concrete structures,
addressing the root cause rather than merely mitigating symptoms.
Recent studies18–22 have provided detailed insight into how MnS
inclusions initiate localized corrosion in carbon and stainless steels. MnS
tends to dissolve preferentially, creating micro-galvanic cells between the
inclusion and surrounding matrix and triggering early pitting. These works
collectively highlight that inclusion chemistry and the local electrochemical
environment govern pit initiation and propagation. Rare earth (RE)

ﬁlm properties through comprehensive electrochemical testing and
immersion experiments in chloride-containing SCP solutions. By eluci-

tance, this work provides critical insights for designing durable
reinforcement materials for marine applications.
Results
Microstructure characterization
Figure 1 presents the metallographic structures of the three steel rebar
specimens, revealing consistent microstructural features dominated by
ferrite-pearlite constituents with minor granular bainite, all exhibiting
characteristic banded morphology. Chromium’s strong ferrite-stabilizing
effect signiﬁcantly reduced pearlite content, while rare earth additions
modiﬁed carbon activity in ferrite, increasing its carbon solubility and
consequently altering the ferrite/pearlite ratio.
Figure 2 shows the morphological and compositional evolution of
inclusions in HRB400 steel following Cr and RE additions. Untreated
HRB400 and Cr-modiﬁed samples contained irregular MnS and Al₂O₃
inclusions along with (Ca,Mg,Al)-O composites (Fig. 2a, b). These inclu-
sions exhibited angular geometries (spherical, reticular, or elongated) with
sharp edges and sizes <2 μm—features known to initiate microcracks and
degrade both mechanical properties and pitting resistance23–25. The types of
inclusions in HRB400-Cr-RE steel bar with RE addition are REAlO3 and
REAlO3 inclusions wrapped by MnS, and the single MnS inclusions
disappear.
To quantify the effect of alloying on inclusion characteristics, a sta-
tistical analysis of inclusion size distribution was conducted based on more
than 100 inclusions per sample. As shown in Fig. S1, Cr and RE additions
markedlyreﬁnedtheinclusions:thefractionofinclusionssmallerthan2 μm
increased sharply, while that of inclusions larger than 5 μm decreased. This
reﬁnement contributes to a more uniform inclusion dispersion and
improved interfacial stability, which are beneﬁcial for corrosion resistance.
Potentiodynamic polarization curves
Figure 3 presents the potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) curves of the
three reinforcing steels in saturated Ca(OH)₂ simulated concrete pore
solution (SCP) with increasing NaCl concentrations, while Table 1
compiles the corresponding pitting potentials (Epit) and passive current
densities (ipass). Irrespective of alloy type, the anodic branches in Fig. 3a–c
enter the passive region directly, without a discernible active–passive
transition, and the virtually superimposed cathodic branches indicate that
oxygen reduction and hydrogen evolution jointly govern the cathodic
kinetics. The congruent proﬁles imply that the underlying corrosion
mechanism remains unchanged despite the Cr/RE alloying; only the
kinetics are altered.
In SCP containing 0.5%, 1%, and 2% Cl⁻, all electrodes anodically
passivate, whereas the cathodic reaction remains charge-transfercontrolled.
ThequantitativedatainTable1revealapronouncedalloyingbeneﬁt.Atlow
chloride concentrations (0.5 wt.%), the passive ﬁlms formed on both steels
remain stable, and the corrosion process is mainly controlled by the passive
ﬁlm’s breakdown potential rather than by inclusion activity. Under these
mild conditions, the Cr–RE effect is lesspronounced. At 1 wt.% Cl⁻ (Fig.3b),
Epit rises from 292 mV for HRB400 to 446 mV for HRB400-Cr and 654 mV

one-third that of HRB400 (38 μA cm⁻²). At 3.5 wt% Cl⁻ (Fig. 3d), the curves
lose the sharp passivation jump, displaying instead a sluggish active-to-
passive transition that signals severe attack on the passive ﬁlm. Even under
this aggressive condition, HRB400-Cr-RE retains the highest Epit and the
lowest ipass (Table 1), underscoring the potent synergy of Cr and RE in
suppressing chloride-induced corrosion.
Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy
Figure 4 presents the impedance spectra (including Nyquist and Bode) for
the three rebar samples exposed to chloride-containing SCP. All Nyquist
traces show single, slightly depressed capacitive arcs, conﬁrming that Cr/RE
micro-alloying modiﬁes only the kinetics while preserving the interfacial
mechanism. The arc radii follow the order HRB400 < HRB400-Cr <
HRB400-Cr-RE, indicating a progressive improvement in charge-transfer
resistance due to the inﬂuence of Cr and RE.
Although the Bode plots show a single dominant time constant, a weak
high-frequency shoulder in the Nyquist plots indicates the contribution of a
surfaceﬁlm.Hence,a two-time-constant equivalent circuitwasemployed to
describe both the ﬁlm and charge-transfer processes. HRB400-Cr-RE
attains phase angles close to 80°, whereas HRB400 barely reaches 70°, and
the impedance modulus follows the same trend. Figure 4b–d reveal that
elevating Cl⁻ from 1 wt% to 3.5 wt% shrinks the arcs, yet the ranking
HRB400-Cr-RE > HRB400-Cr > HRB400 remains intact. Consequently,
Cr/RE alloying systematically enlarges interfacial resistance and markedly
improves chloride tolerance.

Corrosion behavior in Cl-containing simulated concrete
pore ﬂuids
Figure 6 summarises the corrosion rates after immersion for three days.
HRB400 exhibited the highest initial rate (0.12 mm/a), whereas both Cr-
and Cr/RE-modiﬁed steels registered markedly lower values. Over this
period, the Cr/RE alloy reduced the corrosion rate by ~67% relative to
conventional HRB400, reﬂecting the rapid establishment of a dense, Cr/RE-
rich oxide barrier32. This ﬁnding aligns with the potentiodynamic, EIS, and
Mott–Schottky data. As immersion continued, the rates for all steels
declined progressively as additional corrosion products accumulated and
further stabilised the surface33.

After 30 min of exposure (Fig. 8a₁), HRB400 showed extensive matrix
dissolution around the Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO inclusions, producing large
cavity-type pits surrounded by thick corrosion products. Two synergistic
effects drive this acceleration: (i) the interfacial gap that forms as the
inclusion dissolves34 and (ii) lattice distortion arising from the thermal-
expansionmismatchbetweentheinclusionandtheFematrix35.Bothfactors
promote rapid Fe dissolution and pit nucleation.
In HRB400-Cr, the (Ca,Mg,Al)-O complex inclusions remain essen-
tially intact after 30 min (Fig. 8b₁). Their anodic character and limited
chloride adsorption preclude dissolution or detachment, so pitting con-
tinues to be governed by the earlier MnS dissolution rather than by these
oxide particles. HRB400-Cr-RE contains RE–Al–O particles initially
encapsulated by MnS layers (Fig. 8c₁). After 30 min the MnS shell is com-
pletely removed and the RE–Al–O cores begin to dissolve; some inclusions
have vanished entirely, leaving stable pits. Comparison of Fig. 8b₂ (30 min)
with earlier 10-min micrographs conﬁrms that both pit depth and
corrosion-product coverage increase markedly with immersion time.
The results demonstrate that inclusions act as preferential corrosion
initiation sites, with localized matrix dissolution occurring around MnS or
Al₂O₃ particles where surface potential is lower. Once pits nucleate, accu-
mulatedcorrosionproductsformoccludedcellsthatacceleratepropagation.
In contrast, the (Ca,Mg,Al)–O complex inclusions in HRB400-Cr remain
intact after 30 min immersion—neither dissolving nor detaching—thereby
suppressing pit initiation. This stability underpins the superior corrosion
resistance of HRB400-Cr, a conclusion corroborated by both potentiody-
namic and EIS data. In HRB400-Cr-RE, early attack originates from
RE–Al–O–MnS and RE–Al–O inclusions. Rare-earth addition markedly
lowers sulfur content, eliminating discrete MnS; The residual RE-rich
inclusions—whether RE–Al–O or MnS-sheathed RE–Al–O—act as robust
barriers that suppress pit propagation, substantially enhancing the global
corrosion performance of HRB400-Cr-RE.
Observation of corrosion morphology after immersion
Figure 9 presents a comprehensive comparison of the corrosion morphol-
ogies exhibited bythe three rebar grades after3, 7 and 15 days of exposure to
chloride-contaminated simulated concrete pore solution. During the initial
three-day period, HRB400-Cr-RE maintained an almost uniform surface
with only sparse, shallow pits, signifying minimal corrosive attack, whereas
HRB400-Cr already displayed distinct chloride-induced pits highlighted by
yellow arrows, and HRB400 exhibited pronounced rust accumulation. This
morphological hierarchy remained consistent with the weight-loss trends
shown in Fig. 6, corroborating the superior chloride resistance of HRB400-
Cr-RE. As immersion was extended to 7 and 15 days, HRB400 underwent a
progressive transition from localized pitting to generalized corrosion,
accompanied by a marked increase in both the volume and particle size of
corrosion products and a corresponding decline in structural stability. In
contrast, HRB400-Cr exhibited moderate product build-up with enlarging
yet still discrete pits, while HRB400-Cr-RE retained a thin, adherent oxide
layer that intermittently exposed the underlying substrate without excessive
scaling.ThesustainedprotectionaffordedtoHRB400-Cr-REisattributedto
the stabilizing effect of Cr/RE micro-alloying, which promotes a dense, self-
healing passive ﬁlm and effectively suppresses excessive corrosion-product
formation throughout the entire exposure period.
Scanning-laser confocal microscopy (CLSM) was used to assess the
effect of Cr/RE alloying on the early-stage pitting of HRB400 after 3 days in
SCP solutions of varying chloride content; the resulting three-dimensional
topographies are shown in Figs. 10 and S2~ S4 (Supplementary material). In
0.5 wt% NaCl pitting dominated for every steel, yet HRB400 suffered the
most aggressive attack, producing pits as deep as 13.25 μm, whereas
HRB400-Cr-RE displayed the best resistance, with maximum depths lim-
ited to 2.38 μm and the smallest overall damaged area. To quantify the
geometry of these pits, their aspect ratio K (depth/width) was calculated;
higher K denotes sharper, more vertically developed cavities. HRB400
yielded the largest K (up to 0.35, Fig. 10a1), signalling narrow, deeply
penetrating pits, while the alloyed grades exhibited signiﬁcantly lower
values.Depth-distributionhistograms(Fig.10a2–c2)corroboratethistrend:
HRB400pitscentrednear2μm,HRB400-Crnear1μmandHRB400-Cr-RE
mostly below 0.5 μm, conﬁrming that Cr and, especially, Cr/RE additions
markedly suppress pit propagation.
With chloride concentration rising from 0.5 wt% to 3.5 wt%, all three
steels experienced a pronounced mitigation of pitting attack. HRB400, for
instance, saw its deepest cavities drop from 13.25 μm to 3.45 μm, while the
bulk of pits clustered between 0.2 and 0.6 μm. Correspondingly, the max-
imum aspect ratio (K) climbed to ~1.2, signalling a shift toward lateral
enlargement and an increasingly uniform corrosion front. Throughout this
transition, HRB400-Cr-RE retained both the lowest peak K-values and the
shallowest depth distributions, underscoring the efﬁcacy of Cr/RE micro-
alloying in suppressing pit initiation during the earliest stages of chloride
exposure.

chlorides, however, destabilises this ﬁlm and establishes electrochemical

This study investigates the corrosion behavior of Cr/RE-modiﬁed low-
alloy steel rebars in chloride-contaminated simulated concrete pore solu-
tions. In HRB400 steel, MnS and Al₂O₃–MnO–CaO inclusions are trans-
formed into RE–Al–O–S particles with RE addition, and corrosion initiates
withthedissolution of MnS, exposingthe RE–Al–O core and formingstable
pits. RE-modiﬁedinclusions signiﬁcantly delay pit initiation, with HRB400-
Cr-RE showing the highest pitting potential, lowest passive current density,
and largest Nyquist arc radius, along with the lowest corrosion rate and
shallowest pits. Corrosion resistance follows the order HRB400-Cr-RE >
HRB400-Cr > HRB400. As chloride concentration increases, pitting
potential decreases, passive current density rises, capacitive arc radii shrink,
and donor density increases, reﬂecting the progressive dissolution of
inclusions and a corresponding decrease in overall corrosion resistance.
Methods
Materials and solution
Threelow-alloyrebarssamplesare obtainedfromXinxingDuctileIronPipe
Co., Ltd in China. After compositional veriﬁcation in Table 3 by Optical
emission spectrometer (OES), the alloys underwent hot-rolling processing.
The rare earth elements incorporated in this study are cerium (Ce) and
lanthanum (La). For the introduction of these RE elements, a La-Ce alloy
(60 wt% La) was employed. For metallographic analysis, we prepared
10 × 10 × 3 mm³ specimens from rolled plates, etched them in 4 vol.% nital
solution for 5 seconds, then immediately rinsed with ethanol and dried with
compressed air prior to microstructural characterization.
Analysis and characterization of inclusions
For microstructural analysis, 10 × 10 × 10 mm³ steel samples were
sequentially ground (400–4000 grit SiC paper) and mechanically polished,
followed by ultrasonic cleaning in anhydrous ethanol and air drying. We
performed comprehensive characterization using a JEOL JSM-7100F FE-
SEM/EDS system at 30 kV, 10 nA probe current, and 10 mm working
distance, analyzing ≥20 inclusions per sample for statistical reliability.
Quantitative inclusion assessment (size distribution, area fraction) was
conducted using an ASPEX automated analyzer to ensure rigorous
microstructural evaluation.
Electrochemical measurements
Electrochemicalmeasurements were performed usinga conventionalthree-
electrode system, with the steel reinforcement specimens (10 × 10 × 5 mm3)
serving as the working electrode (1 cm2 exposed area). A platinum sheet and
saturated calomel electrode (SCE) were employed as the counter and
reference electrodes, respectively. Prior to testing, the working electrodes
were encapsulated in epoxy resin to ensure geometric consistency, then
sequentially ground to 2000-grit using silicon carbide abrasive paper. To
eliminate surface contaminants, the specimens were ultrasonically cleaned
in anhydrous ethanol and deionized water, followed by drying under con-
trolled nitrogen ﬂow to prevent oxidation.
The electrochemical tests were conducted in a simulated concrete
pore solution (saturated Ca(OH)₂, pH ~12.5) at 25 ± 1 °C. The chloride
concentration was systematically varied (0–3.5 wt% NaCl) to evaluate
corrosion behavior under different aggressive conditions. Open-circuit
potential (OCP) monitoring was ﬁrst performed until stabilization
(±2 mV/h variation), followed by potentiodynamic polarization tests. The
