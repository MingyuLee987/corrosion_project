Effects of Modified Inclusions and Precipitates Alloyed by Rare Earth 
Element on Corrosion and Impact Properties in Low Alloy Steel
Xin‑Tong Lian1,2 · Long Chen1,2 · Zeng‑Wei Fan1,2 · Teng‑Shi Liu1,2 · De‑Xiang Xu2 · Han Dong1,2
Received: 3 November 2021 / Revised: 29 December 2021 / Accepted: 10 January 2022 / Published online: 5 May 2022

Abstract
This study has focused on the morphology and distribution of inclusions and precipitates modified by rare earth (RE) ele-
ments, which has a decisive influence on microstructure, corrosion properties and impact behaviors in Q355 low alloy steel. 
Characterized by the method of electrolytic extraction and ASPEX-scanning electron microscopy (ASPEX-SEM), small-
sized spherical RE inclusions have been modified to replace elongated MnS and large-sized ­Al2O3. Q355RE steel after RE 
alloying has lower corrosion rate and higher value of α/γ, due to the formation of stable and dense rust layers. Q355RE steel 
also exhibits better resistance to fracture at low temperature, owing to the presence of RE modification to inclusions and 
its effects on reducing crack initiation and propagation. Nano-scale RE precipitates containing sulfur and phosphorus is 
observed along grain boundaries by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The purification of grain boundaries by RE 
is beneficial to the improvement of corrosion and impact properties.
Keywords  Rare earth element · Inclusion · Precipitate · Q355 low alloy steel · Corrosion resistance · Impact toughness
1  Introduction
Steel for civil construction is a mainstream trend of steel 
usage in China. Compared with traditional brick-wood and 
concrete structures, steel structure possesses light weight and 
low energy consumption, and more than 60% of steel materi-
als can be recycled after demolition [1–3]. Q355 low alloy 
steel has been one of the most widely used building materi-
als in steel structure for a long time [4–6]. However, Q355 
steel is usually subjected to atmospheric corrosion related to 
various factors such as air temperature, humidity, sunshine 
and pollutant, which may lead to corrosion structure failure 
[7, 8]. Two common methods are used to alleviate destruc-
tive corrosion including multi-alloying and coating. From 
the perspective of economy and environmental protection, 
a cheaper and environment-friendly technology is urgent to 
be developed. Another problem that all structural steels may 
face is low temperature structure failure. When the ambient 
temperature is declined to a lower temperature, the brittle-
ness of Q355 steel increases significantly, which probably 
leads to cold-brittle failure [9–11]. Impact toughness test 
is an effective method to evaluate the ability of material to 
resist external impact.
Is there any low-cost way to solve the two problems dis-
cussed above? Researchers have indicated that large-sized 
inclusions are the main causes to corrosion and impact fail-
ure [12, 13]. Large-sized inclusions such as ­Al2O3 and MnS 
are easy to be formed in structural steels during solidifica-
tion due to the segregation of chemical compositions. It is 
well-known that large-sized inclusions are hard to be elimi-
nated and always provide initiation sites for pitting corrosion 
and cracks [14–16]. Some researchers have focused on the 
advantageous roles of rare earth (RE) elements adding into 
structural steels. La and Ce are the left-over elements in 
the extraction of RE elements in China and are not expen-
sive. After adding Ce and La into steels, the morphology 
and size of inclusions have been changed significantly and 
stable precipitates containing RE including oxides, sulfides 
and oxysulfides tend to be formed more readily [17–20].

2  Experimental
2.1  Specimens Design
A common commercial Q355 low alloy steel was chosen as 
the experimental material. The metal used for RE alloying 
was Ce and La mischmetal with the mass ratio of about 1:2. 
Two slabs in 230 mm thick were heated to 1240 °C for 5 h. 
After air-cooling to 1070 °C, followed by rolling via seven 
passes on a rolling mill, the slabs were rolled to plates of 
35 mm thickness. The finish rolling was controlled at 880 °C 
and the final plates were rolled to 11.75 mm. Subsequently, 
the plate was hot-rolled at 660 °C and air cooled to room 
temperature. The chemical compositions of the two struc-
tural steels under investigation are listed in Table 1.
2.2  Test Procedure
Metallographic images and inclusion morphology were 
observed by optical microscopy (OM) and scanning electron 
microscopy (SEM) combined with energy dispersive X-ray 
spectroscopy (EDS). Inclusion identifying was processed 
by electrolytic extraction methods and SEM to observe 
the three-dimensional morphology directly. The schematic 
illustration of an electrolytic apparatus used for separating 
inclusions is show in Fig. 1. The electrolytic solution was 
composed of 30 g ­FeSO4, 10 g NaCl and 20 g ­C6H6Na2O7 
dissolved in 1000 mL distilled water. After electrolysis pro-
cess, inclusions were obtained by centrifugal separation and 
fully dried to observe the morphology. ASPEX-SEM was 
used to detect on the area of 100 ­mm2 randomly in the sam-
ple to characterize inclusions. Three samples were analyzed 
and average value of statistical results was taken in results.
Three parallel samples (55 mm × 50 mm × 3 mm) were 
prepared for evaluating the rate of corrosion weight loss by 
the method of wet-dry immersion test. Before immersion test 
was undertaken, samples were cleaned by supersonic cleaner 
and weighed after fully dry. Then samples were exposed to 
a solution of 0.01 mol/L ­NaHSO3 at 45 °C. The accelerated 
corrosion durations were determined at 12 h, 24 h, 48 h and 
72 h according to the guideline of TB/T 1979–2014. The 
corrosion products on the surface of samples were removed 
by acid solution and the samples were weighed to determine 
the mass loss.
Charpy V-notch impact test was conducted with the sam-
ples of 10 mm × 10 mm × 55 mm. The samples were cut 
along the rolling direction, and the impact toughness data 
presented is an average of three measurements for each test 
condition. The transverse and longitudinal toughness were 
carried out at gradient temperature from − 60 to 0 °C. The 
fractograph and dislocation morphology were observed by 
SEM–EDS and transmission electron microscopy (TEM), 
respectively.

4  Discussion
4.1  Change of Microstructure
The change of microstructure proves that RE probably 
impedes the diffusion of carbon and solute atoms and 
increase the content of ferrite correspondingly, which is 
similar to the conclusion obtained by previous works [10, 
22–24]. During the cooling process after hot rolling, the pre-
eutectoid ferrite precipitates from austenite when cooling 
temperature reaches Ar3. When cooling temperature contin-
ues to drop below the temperature of Ar1, eutectoid decom-
position occurs and cement ­(Fe3C) appears. The formation 
of ­Fe3C reduces the surrounding carbon concentration and 
promotes the nucleation of ferrite. Then ferrite continues 
to nucleate and grow on both sides of ­Fe3C, and these two 
phases are integrated into pearlite in general. Due to the fast 
cooling rate, a small amount of bainite precipitates from the 
supersaturated ferrite matrix in the final state.
By comparing the metallographic structure of Q355 steel 
and Q355RE steel, it is obvious that RE elements can pro-
mote the formation of ferrite. During the growth of ferrite, 
carbon and other solute atoms concentrate into austenite and 
cause the transformation from retained austenite into pearlite 
or bainite [22]. When the temperature drops below trans-
formation temperature of Bs, bainite is formed. However, 
the diffusion of RE as a solute atom is quite difficult due to 
its large atomic radius (Ce 0.1877 nm, La 0.1825 nm, Fe 
0.1210 nm) [21]. The interaction coefficient between RE and 
carbon has been reported to be negatives [25].
4.2  Modification of Inclusions
RE element plays an important role on the shape control 
and modification of inclusions in Q355 steel, as shown 
in Figs. 3 and 4. The statistical results of various types 
of inclusions are listed in Table 2. A total number of 368 
inclusions are detected. The proportion of sulfides includ-
ing isolated MnS and complex sulfides is the highest, 
accounting for 59% of all detected inclusions. The other 
inclusions are mainly complex inclusions consisted of 
oxides ­(Al2O3) and sulfides (MnS and CaS). The inclu-
sion size of MnS is the largest among all the inclusions 
with the average inclusion size of 4.7 μm. As a contrast, 
362 inclusions were detected in the sample of Q355RE 
steel. The type of inclusions transfers from common 
oxides and sulfides to RE sulfide, RE oxide or RE com-
plex inclusions. The amount of RE sulfide is the largest 
and accounts for 48.3% among all the inclusions with 
the average size of 2.7 μm. Due to the active chemical 
property of RE elements, it is concluded that RE ele-
ments tend to react with oxygen and sulfur firstly, which 
coincides with previous study focused on thermodynamic 
calculation results [19, 25].
By comparison, it is found that sulfide inclusions account 
for more than 50% in Q355 steel and the number of inclu-
sions in Q355 steel is largely determined by the sulfur con-
tent of steel. The size distribution of inclusions is statisti-
cally evaluated and demonstrated in Fig. 7. It can be seen 
that the size of most inclusions in Q355 steel ranges from 
1.5 to 3.5 μm, accounting for about 80% of all inclusions 
(Fig. 7a). The maximum size of inclusions is more than 
10 μm, and inclusions with large size are mainly consisted of 
MnS and ­Al2O3 (Fig. 7b). After RE alloying, the maximum 
size of inclusion is less than 7.5 μm, and the average size of

4.3  Mechanism of Corrosion Resistance 
Improvement
Corrosion rate is closely related to the formation of rust lay-
ers on sample surface. The morphology of oxide products 
on the surface of rust layers is demonstrated in Fig. 8. The 
circular corrosion morphology is formed on the surface of 
samples under cycled wet-dry immersion tests, which is due 
to the deposition of liquid on the surface unevenly. It is obvi-
ous that the corrosion uniformity of Q355RE steel is much 
better than that of Q355 steel. The rust layer particles turn 
finer in Q355RE steel after RE alloying. A small amount 
of oxide particles containing RE is found on the surface of 
rust layer, as shown in the red circle in Fig. 8d. RE oxide 
particles also contain other alloying elements such as Al and 
Si, indicating that RE inclusions participate in the corrosion 
reaction of Q355RE steel.
By analyzing the XRD patterns of corrosion prod-
ucts, the main phases consist of γ-FeOOH, α-FeOOH and 
­Fe3O4/γ-Fe2O3 (Fig. 9). Based on previous study, it is known 
that unstable γ-FeOOH is an electrochemical active phase 
and is formed in the initial stage of corrosion, which can 
transform into ­Fe3O4/γ-Fe2O3 and α-FeOOH during the cor-
rosion process [27, 28]. Stable α-FeOOH is the most protec-
tive hydroxy iron oxide and prevents corrosive ions from 
invading the matrix. ­Fe3O4 and γ-Fe2O3 have the similar 
XRD patterns and it is quite difficult to distinguish accu-
rately whether it is a mixture or a single compound.
Table 3 shows the relative content of different corrosion 
products. The content of stable α-FeOOH is higher and the 
content of unstable γ-FeOOH is lower in Q355RE steel com-
pared to those in Q355 steel. The content of ­Fe3O4/γ-Fe2O3 
is decreased slightly. The ratio of α-FeOOH/γ-FeOOH (α/γ)

active area at the surface of steel and weakens micro‐region 
electrochemical corrosion. RE inclusions have also exhibited 
a much lower Volta potential than the matrix. Furthermore, 
some oxide particles containing RE is found on the surface 
of rust layer, indicating that RE inclusions participate in the 
corrosion reaction of Q355RE steel. Therefore, RE inclu-
sions dissolve and precipitate during corrosion process and 
part of them exist in the form of insoluble RE compounds 
in the rust layers.

Impact fracture analysis of transverse samples is per-
formed to display the morphology of impact fracture, as 
shown in Fig. 11. Impact properties are a combination 
evaluation of strength and plasticity and notched impact 
samples are more sensitive to the variation of micro-
structure [30, 31]. The main reason for increasing the 
impact energy after RE alloying can be attributed to the 
modification of inclusions. Angular inclusions may lead 
to micro-cracks initiation under loading and large-sized 
inclusions are detrimental to mechanical properties due 
to the difference of hardness and thermal expansion prop-
erties between matrix and inclusions. It is noted that the 
presence of inclusion (MnS, ­Al2O3) act as potential sites 
for crack initiation [32, 33]. It demonstrates that plastic 
deformation occurs in matrix and cracks begin to initi-
ate and propagate at both ends of large inclusions when 
the load is applied in Q355 steel. Then the internal and 
boundary cracks occur around inclusion. Ultimately, the 
inclusions are separated from the matrix and the whole 
inclusion is regarded as the crack source, as shown in 
Fig. 11a. The larger the size of inclusions, the faster the 
rate of crack propagation after plastic deformation. When 
RE elements is added into steels, sulfur and RE elements 
act to form sub-micro spherical RE sulfides and composite 
inclusions ­(Al2O3 + MnS + CaS), which replaces striplike 
MnS. The shape of RE inclusions is mostly spherical or 
ellipsoidal with good morphology and distribution. Com-
pared to sulfide inclusions, the linear expansion coeffi-
cient and elastic modulus of RE inclusions are similar 
to those in the matrix, which can effectively reduce the 
stress concentration around inclusions during impact test 
[34]. Small spherical RE inclusions have good cohesion 
with matrix and are less likely to be the crack sources, as 
indicated in Fig. 11b. It has been reported that RE inclu-
sions with certain size range can produce large plastic 
deformation during the impact test and can alter the path 
of crack growth [34, 35]. Simultaneously, the activity of

detrimental impurity element due to its tendency to seg-

of S on the mechanical properties has been investigated. 
According to the theory of grain boundary segregation, the 
impurity elements such as S, P and Sb segregate towards 
grain boundaries, which weaken grain boundaries and 
leads to brittle fracture [39, 40]. RE has been reported 
to the depressing deleterious effects of low melting point 
metals in the way of fixation of tramp elements and mini-
mizing amount of Fe–P phase [18]. This could be provided 
as a further evidence that stress concentration caused by 
element segregation is eliminated by purification of grain 
boundaries, leading to the enhancement of impact tough-
ness. S can also gather at grain boundaries to inhibit uni-
form corrosion rate and is more likely to cause pitting 
corrosion [41]. Grain boundary purification is helpful to 
improve both impact toughness and corrosion properties.
Generally, nano-scale precipitate combines well with 
the matrix, and no split between precipitate and matrix 
occurs during impact test. Furthermore, the segregation 
of RE at grain boundaries and the interactions with other 
elements should be considered. More works need to fur-
ther study whether a direct correlation between nano-scale 
precipitate and mechanical properties.
5  Conclusions
1.	 The microstructure of Q355RE steel was consisted of 
ferrite and pearlite and that of Q355 steel consisted of 
ferrite and bainite, which was due to the effect of RE on 
transformation temperature. Small-sized spherical RE 
inclusions (RES, ­RE2O3) replaced elongated MnS and 
large-sized ­Al2O3 in Q355RE steel after modifying by 
RE elements.
2.	 Compared to Q355 steel, Q355RE steel had lower corro-
sion rate. The value of α/γ was increased after RE alloy-
ing, indicating that more stable oxide products were 
formed on the surface of Q355RE steel. The decrease 
in corrosion rate was closely related to the formation of 
stable and dense rust layers promoted by RE alloying.
3.	 Q355RE steel also exhibited better resistance to fracture 
at low temperature, owing to the presence of RE modi-
fication to inclusions and its effects on reducing crack 
initiation.
4.	 RE precipitates containing sulfur and phosphorus was 
observed along grain boundaries. The purification of 
grain boundaries by RE was probably beneficial to the 
improvement of corrosion and impact properties.
