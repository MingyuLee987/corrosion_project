Micro-Electrochemical Properties of CeS Inclusions in Stainless
Steel and Inhibiting Effects of Ce3+ Ions on Pitting
Masashi Nishimoto,∗,z Izumi Muto,∗∗,z Yu Sugawara,∗∗and Nobuyoshi Hara∗∗
Department of Materials Science, Tohoku University, Aramaki, Aoba-ku, Sendai 980-8579, Japan
Micro-scale polarization data from small surface areas of stainless steels with either CeS or MnS inclusions was measured in NaCl
and Na2SO4 solutions to elucidate the dissolution and pit initiation behavior of both types of inclusions. Stable pitting was initiated
at the CeS inclusion at potentials which exceeded the dissolution potential region of the CeS inclusion, whereas a stable pit at the
MnS inclusion was initiated in the dissolution potential range of the MnS inclusion. Thermodynamic calculations indicated that the
Ce3+ ions are likely to be produced by the dissolution of the CeS inclusion. In the micro-scale polarization of a small area with a
MnS inclusion, stable pitting at the MnS inclusion was inhibited in NaCl solutions containing Ce3+. The formation of a shallow
trench was observed at the MnS inclusion/steel matrix boundary in the Ce3+-containing solution, whereas a deep trench was formed
in the Ce3+-free solution. It is suggested that the Ce3+ ions inhibit trench formation at the MnS/steel matrix boundary, resulting in
improved pitting corrosion resistance at sulﬁde inclusions.

While stainless steels are successfully used in corrosive environ-
ments, they sometimes suffer from pitting. Improving the pitting cor-
rosion resistance of stainless steels is of the utmost importance because
of the vital role these steels play in infrastructure, transportation, en-
ergy, in bridges, and buildings in general. The pit initiation sites for
commercial stainless steels have been attributed to sulﬁde inclusions
such as MnS in Cl−-containing environments,1–10 and hence consid-
erable research has been carried out to clarify the mechanism of the
pitting at MnS inclusions. It has been proposed that the initial step of
the pit initiation process at MnS inclusions is the anodic dissolution
of the inclusions and that the complete dissolution of the inclusions is
not required for pit initiation.11–21 The release of S-containing species
during MnS dissolution increases pitting susceptibility at and around
the inclusions. Webb and Alkire demonstrated that the dissolution of
the inclusions produces S2O32−ions and leads to weak acidiﬁcation
near the inclusions.14 Chiba et al. proposed that the synergistic effect
of chloride ions and dissolution products from MnS causes trench
formation at the boundary of the inclusion/steel matrix, and pitting
occurs at MnS inclusions as a local transition from the passive to the
active state of the steel matrix inside the trench.19 The effect of mi-
nor elements on the pitting at MnS inclusions has been investigated.
Lillard et al. proposed that Cu ions are released from the steel matrix
inside the trench and MnS inclusions are ennobled by Cu deposition.21
They suggested that the ennobled MnS inclusions accelerate pit prop-
agation inside the trench. It is therefore reasonable to assume that the
inhibition of the dissolution of the inclusion will directly suppress
the formation of trenches, resulting in an improvement in the pitting
corrosion resistance of stainless steels.
The chemical composition of sulﬁde inclusions has a signiﬁcant
inﬂuence on pitting corrosion behavior. It has been reported that Cr-
sulﬁde, Ti-sulﬁde, and Ti-carbosulﬁde inclusions have better pitting
corrosion resistance than MnS inclusions.22–30 Muto et al. and Shima-
hashi et al. revealed that the oxide-enriched layers generated on the
inclusion surfaces prevent the anodic dissolution of the inclusions
and improve pitting corrosion resistance.25,29 It is also reported that
Ce-sulﬁde and oxysulﬁde inclusions have higher pitting corrosion re-
sistance than MnS inclusions.31–34 Because it is a rare-earth element,
Ce is relatively reactive in aqueous environments. While the pitting
corrosion resistance of CrS and TiS inclusions is attributed to the
thermodynamic stability of the surface oxide ﬁlms on the inclusions,
the stability of the surface oxides on CeS inclusions is unlikely to be

higher than that of CrS or TiS. The pitting corrosion resistance of CeS
inclusions is therefore the result of other factors and is possibly related
to the Ce-species produced by the dissolution of the inclusions.
Cerium has been shown to be effective in the corrosion inhibition
of Al alloys.35–40 Hinton et al. indicated that the corrosion rate of Al al-
loys decreased in NaCl solutions with the addition of Ce salts.35 They
found that the oxygen reduction reaction is inhibited by the formation
of Ce-containing oxide layers on the cathodic sites. The corrosion inhi-
bition for stainless steels by Ce-species has also been investigated.41–45
Lu and Ives demonstrated that Ce chemical treatment improves the
crevice corrosion resistance of stainless steels.41,42 Also in the case
of stainless steels, Ce chemical treatment is expected to inhibit the
cathodic reactions. It is therefore predicted that Ce-species released
from CeS inclusions affect the pitting corrosion resistance of stainless
steels. However, little is known about the electrochemical properties
of CeS inclusions, and the relationship between those properties and
pitting corrosion resistance remains unclear.
In this paper, the micro-scale polarization of small surface areas
of stainless steels with either CeS or MnS inclusions was measured
in NaCl and Na2SO4 solutions to elucidate the dissolution and pit
initiation behavior of the inclusions. The potential-pH diagram for the
Ce-S-CeS-H2O system was calculated to determine the dissolution
products from the CeS inclusion. The effect of Ce ions on the pitting
corrosion resistance at MnS inclusions was investigated in NaCl-
CeCl3 solutions. Finally, the role of Ce ions in improving the pitting
corrosion resistance of the sulﬁde inclusions is discussed based on the
micro-scale polarization and the cross-sectional morphology of the
inclusions after polarization.
Experimental
Specimens.—Two types of stainless steels were prepared by vac-
uum induction melting (50-kg ingot) and then were hot-rolled. The
chemical compositions of the steels are listed in Table I. In steel A,
Mn and S were added to form MnS inclusions. In steel B, Ce and S
were added to form CeS inclusions. These steels were not deoxidized
by Al to avoid the formation of Al-rich inclusions.46 The steels were
cut into specimens with dimensions of 15 mm × 25 mm × 5 mm.
All the specimens were heat-treated at 1373 K for 0.5 h and quenched
in water. After heat-treatment, the specimens were abraded with a
series of SiC papers up to 1500 grit and then polished by a diamond
paste down to 1 μm. The specimens were ﬁnally rinsed with ethanol.
Since the non-metallic inclusions are darker in color under an optical
microscope compared to the stainless steel matrix, they are clearly
visible in the as-polished condition without etching.

Micro-scale anodic polarization.—Potentiodynamic anodic po-
larization was carried out in 0.1 M NaCl and 0.1 M Na2SO4 solutions.
The pH values of the 0.1 M NaCl and 0.1 M Na2SO4 solutions were
5.5 and 5.8, respectively. In addition to these solutions, NaCl-CeCl3
and Na2SO4-Ce2(SO4)3 solutions were also used. The chemical com-
positions and pH values of the solutions are shown in Table II. The
solutions were prepared from deionized water and analytical grade
chemicals. All measurements were performed at 298 K under natu-
rally aerated conditions. To make a micro-scale electrode area con-
taining a single inclusion, the specimen surfaces were masked by
a vinyl copolymerized resin (Sunhayato VA-30). The electrode area
was ca. 100 μm × 100 μm. Since this resin is hydrophilic, it is easy
that solution was placed on the specimen without leaving air bubbles
on the electrode surface. An acrylic cell was placed on the masked
specimen to preserve the solution. A small Ag/AgCl reference elec-
trode and a Pt wire counter electrode were ﬁxed to the acrylic cell.
All the potentials reported in this study refer to the Ag/AgCl (3.33 M
KCl) electrode (0.206 V vs. standard hydrogen electrode at 298 K).
A battery-powered potentiostat17 was used to control the electrode
potential of the specimen with a scanning rate of 3.83 × 10−4 V s−1
(23 mV min−1). Although slower scan rates are reliable for poten-
tiodynamic polarization in general (e.g., 1.67 × 10−4 V s−1 (10 mV
min−1)),47 the higher scan rate was used to shorten the experiment
time and to reduce the data capacity of the in situ observation system
described below.
During polarization, the electrode area was observed by the
in situ real-time optical micro-scale observation system. The de-
tails of the system developed by Chiba et al. have been published
elsewhere.18 The micro-electrochemical cell was put on the stage
of an Olympus BX51M optical microscope. An Olympus LUMPlan
Fl 100XW water immersion objective lens was used to obtain the
real-time video images of the micro-scale electrode surface during
polarization.

Results and Discussion
Inclusion characterization.—The chemical composition and mor-
phology of the inclusions were characterized using an optical micro-
scope and the FE-SEM/EDS. Figure 1 shows the optical micrographs,
SEM images, and EDS maps of the inclusions. Table III shows the
results of the EDS analysis at each point in Fig. 1. In steel A (the
Ce-free specimen), as shown in Fig. 1a, the inclusion was elongated
and the surface was gray in the optical micrograph. On the other hand,
in Fig. 1b, the inclusion in steel B (the Ce-containing specimen) was
round and the surface was orange and dark gray, with light gray areas
in the optical micrograph. The chemical composition of the inclusions
affects their morphology and surface color. In Fig. 1a, enriched Mn
and S were present in the inclusion in steel A. The relative atomic
percentage of Mn:S:Cr:O at point “A” was 48:47:5:<1, indicating the
formation of the inclusions composed of Mn, S, and a small amount
of Cr (approximately 5 at.%). This is the typical sulﬁde inclusion in
commercial type 304 stainless steels and is referred to as MnS in this
paper.
In the optical micrograph of steel B, the inclusion consisted of
orange, dark gray, and light gray areas. The orange and dark gray areas
corresponded to the bright area (points “B1” and “B2”) in the SEM
image. The light gray area (point “B3”) in the optical micrograph was
darker than points “B1” and “B2” in the SEM image. The EDS maps
show that the orange and dark gray areas (“B1” and “B2”) were rich in
O, and the S content was relatively low. In contrast, the enrichment of
S and a small amount of Cr and O was detected in the light gray area
(“B3”). As Table III shows, the relative atomic percentage of Ce:S:O
at point “B1” was 41:16:43. There was little difference in the chemical
compositions between point “B1” and “B2”. It was suggested that the
orange and dark gray areas in the optical micrograph were Ce2O2S
(cerium oxysulﬁde). It is possible that small difference in the O content
between point “B1” and “B2” led to the different colors of the Ce2O2S
inclusion. It has been reported that the color of cerium oxide varies
with the oxidation state.48 The light gray area in the optical micrograph
was mainly composed of Ce and S even though small amounts of Cr
and O were contained in the inclusion. The relative atomic percentage
of Ce:S:Cr:O at point “B3” was 32:48:15:5, indicating the formation
of complex sulﬁde of Ce and Cr. There was a small enrichment of Ca in
the right edge of the light gray area. For simplicity, the light gray area
of the inclusion in steel B is referred as CeS in this paper. The Ce2O2S
is thought to be a deoxidation product, and the CeS was produced on
the Ce2O2S surface in the solidiﬁcation process of the steel. Since Al
was not added to avoid the formation of Al-rich inclusions, Ce acted
as a deoxidation agent. CeS could be distinguished from Ce2O2S
without EDS analysis because the different colors of CeS and Ce2O2S
made it possible to visually discern one from the other in the optical
micrographs.

area with the MnS inclusion in steel A measured in 0.1 M NaCl.

Interestingly, a comparison of the pitting and the CeS inclusion
dissolution potentials (Figs. 4 and 9) indicates that the pit initia-
tion potential at the CeS inclusion was considerably higher than the
dissolution potential of the CeS inclusion. In the case of MnS in-
clusions, it is well-known that stable pitting is initiated in the dis-
solution potential range of MnS inclusions.12–14,17,18 MnS dissolu-
tion directly results in the formation of a trench around the inclu-
sions, and the meta-stable and stable pitting events proceed after
the trench is formed. On the other hand, the pit initiation potential
at the CeS inclusion is approximately 0.3 V higher than the dis-

Conclusions
1.
A stable pit was generated at the CeS inclusion at 0.8 V in natu-
rally aerated 0.1 M NaCl (298 K). The pitting potential at the CeS
inclusion was 0.4 V higher than that of the MnS inclusion. How-
ever, the pit initiation site, which was the inclusion/steel matrix
boundary, was found to be common to both these inclusions.
2.
In the case of the MnS inclusion, a stable pit was initiated in
the potential range of the inclusion dissolution. The dissolution
of the CeS inclusion proceeded at approximately 0.5 V, but pit
initiation was suppressed until 0.8 V. The difference in the pitting
and dissolution potential can likely be attributed to the inhibitive
effect of Ce-species on pitting. On the basis of thermodynamic
calculations, it is suggested that the Ce3+ ions were released from
CeS inclusions.
3.
The Ce3+ ions improved the pitting corrosion resistance at the
MnS inclusion in 0.1 M and 3 M NaCl solutions. In the micro-
scale polarization of a small area with a MnS inclusion, a stable
pit was initiated in Ce3+-free solutions, whereas no stable pit was
generated in Ce3+-containing solutions even when metastable pit-
ting events were observed. In the Ce3+-free solution, the boundary
between the MnS inclusion and the steel matrix dissolved selec-
tively, and a deep trench was formed. On the other hand, only a
shallow trench was formed in the Ce3+-containing solution. This
suggests that the Ce3+ ions inhibit dissolution at the MnS/steel
boundary, resulting in the improved pitting corrosion resistance
of sulﬁde inclusions in stainless steels.
